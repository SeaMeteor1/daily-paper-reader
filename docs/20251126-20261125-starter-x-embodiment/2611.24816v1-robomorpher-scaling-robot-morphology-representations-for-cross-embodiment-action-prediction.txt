Abstract
Learning shared action representations is central to cross-
embodiment manipulation. However, identical joint values
can produce different spatial configurations across robots,
limiting representations learned from joint values alone.
We introduce RoboMorpher, a morphology-conditioned ac-
tion tokenizer that jointly reconstructs joint angles and link
poses to associate control coordinates with their kinematic
effects. MorphGen supplies scalable kinematic supervision
by generating serial-arm descriptions from survey-derived
geometric priors.
We pretrain RoboMorpher on 40 mil-
lion morphology–state pairs and integrate the frozen to-
kenizer into a vision–language–action policy for discrete
prediction and continuous refinement. We also introduce
EmbodiBench, a ten-task benchmark with 30 generated
URDFs per task. With downstream URDFs excluded from
tokenizer pretraining, the policy achieves 56.40% success
on RoboTwin 2.0 and 23.27% on EmbodiBench, exceeding
continuous joint-space control by 9.73 and 15.34 percent-
age points. Removing link-pose supervision reduces Em-
bodiBench success by 17.94 percentage points, supporting
morphology-aware geometric supervision for joint-action
representation learning.
1. Introduction
Generalist robot policies learn from demonstrations across
platforms [11, 13, 14], but the kinematic meaning of joint-
space actions depends on robot morphology. Identical joint
values can produce different link poses as link lengths and
joint arrangements vary; padding actions to a common di-
mension does not resolve this ambiguity.
End-effector
(EE) control provides Cartesian targets but requires robot-
specific inverse kinematics and does not uniquely specify
the full joint configuration. A shared joint-action represen-
tation should therefore preserve control coordinates while
accounting for their morphology-dependent spatial effects.
To learn this relationship without collecting manipula-
tion demonstrations for every morphology, we use robot
kinematics as supervision. We introduce MorphGen, a pro-
cedural generator informed by a survey of 33 URDF/MJCF
descriptions. MorphGen normalizes serial-arm kinematics
into a common local-Z convention, samples six- and seven-
joint manipulators from empirical link-length priors, and se-
lects feasible home poses through collision-aware inverse
kinematics. Forward kinematics provides link-pose targets
for 40 million morphology–state pairs.
We present RoboMorpher, a morphology-conditioned
action tokenizer pretrained on these data.
Morphology
conditions its encoder and decoder, while kinematic-chain
attention biases encode relative joint positions along the
chain.
Joint-angle reconstruction preserves control coor-
dinates, and link-pose reconstruction supervises their kine-
matic effects. The pretrained tokenizer is then frozen and
integrated into a vision–language–action (VLA) policy. Fol-
lowing Discrete Forcing [21], a discrete branch predicts ac-
tion codes, which are decoded into coarse trajectories and
refined by a continuous flow-matching branch. Pretrained
morphology features condition both branches.
We evaluate on 15 RoboTwin 2.0 tasks across five em-
bodiments [3] and introduce EmbodiBench, comprising ten
tabletop tasks with 30 generated URDFs each. Downstream
URDFs are excluded from tokenizer pretraining; policies
learn from task demonstrations.
RoboMorpher achieves
56.40% and 23.27% success, exceeding continuous joint-
space control by 9.73 and 15.34 percentage points. Remov-
ing link-pose supervision reduces EmbodiBench success to
5.33%, supporting its contribution to downstream perfor-
mance.

3. Method
Our pipeline separates kinematic representation learning
from task-specific action selection (Fig. 2).
MorphGen
generates morphology–configuration pairs with forward-
kinematics (FK) supervision. RoboMorpher encodes con-
figurations into joint-aligned codes and reconstructs angles
and link poses conditioned on morphology. A VLA policy
predicts these codes and refines decoded trajectories while
keeping the tokenizer frozen.

3.1.1. Robot Morphology Survey and Geometric Priors
We survey 33 serial-manipulator descriptions covering 30
robot names. Gen3, Koch v1.1, and Panda each have URDF
and MJCF entries. Six- and seven-DoF arms account for
30/33 descriptions (90.9%), motivating our sampling range.
Variation in joint limits and tool coupling motivates explicit
arm-chain extraction and separate gripper handling. Table 1
and Sec. A summarize the inventory and description ambi-
guities.
A separate set of 30 link-length records contains 185 re-
ported values across seven joint positions (Fig. 1(a)). The
positions have 30, 30, 30, 30, 30, 27, and 8 observations,
respectively.
Zeros are retained and missing entries ex-
cluded. Positions 3 and 4 have the largest means (0.3029
and 0.2270 m), while position 5 has the largest sample
standard deviation (0.1862 m). These differences motivate
position-specific Gaussian priors. The priors characterize
the surveyed inventory, with only eight observations sup-
porting the seventh position.

3.3. VLA Policy Integration
To use the pretrained representation for control, we com-
bine Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct [1], the frozen tokenizer, and
a two-branch diffusion Transformer (DiT). Given images I,
instruction ℓ, state s, and morphology M, the policy pre-
dicts 50-frame chunks of absolute joint positions and grip-
per commands. Each arm occupies seven padded joint slots
and one gripper channel, giving 16 coordinates for two arms.
Masks exclude absent joints and arms.
3.3.1. From Frame Tokens to Action Chunks
To apply the frame-level tokenizer to action chunks, we
fit K = 10 nodes to each arm’s 50-frame trajectory A ∈
R50×J. With endpoint-aligned interpolation matrix U ∈
R50×10, ridge fitting yields B∗∈R10×J,
B∗= (U ⊤U + 10−4I)−1U ⊤A.
(11)
Each fitted node is tokenized independently. Decoded nodes
are interpolated to 50 frames at inference. Grippers use lin-
ear resampling and 256-bin quantization. An arm with J
joints contributes K(2J +1) indices, giving at most 300 for
two seven-joint arms.
3.3.2. Morphology-Conditioned Policy Architecture
LayerNorm and a linear map project each VLM hidden-
state level into width-1024, 16-head action blocks. The L
action blocks align with the VLM layers and alternate VLM
cross-attention with action self-attention. Following the par-
tially shared design of Discrete Forcing [21], our branches
share two initial blocks and use separate remaining blocks
and output heads. The branches do not exchange attention.
Current state and 32 learned future queries provide context,
while frozen URDF features modulate each branch through
AdaLN. The VLM instruction additionally includes 256-bin
state tokens.

valid action codes, using a cosine masking ratio determined
by a uniformly sampled time. At inference, eight MaskGIT
iterations [2] commit codes by confidence. Frozen decoding

4. EmbodiBench Evaluation
EmbodiBench evaluates whether the learned representation
supports manipulation across generated kinematic chains in
SAPIEN. Its ten tasks comprise three lifting, three picking,
three stacking, and one pushing variant, with single arms or
same-/opposite-side dual arms (Fig. 3).
Data and coverage.
Each task uses 30 URDFs and
50 demonstrations per task–URDF pair, totaling 15,000
demonstrations. The overlapping task-specific sets contain
55 distinct generated morphologies. Dual-arm trials use the
same morphology for both arms. Policies receive head and
wrist RGB views resized to 224×224. Absent arm channels
are masked.
Evaluation.
Evaluation uses separate scene seeds and five
trials per task–URDF pair: 150 per task and 1,500 per
method. Scenes rejected by the expert planner do not con-
sume the quota. Success rates are therefore conditional on
planner feasibility. Each accepted trial has a fixed instruc-
tion and permits 300 executed action commands, counted
per frame within a chunk. We report task success rates and
their unweighted mean.
Success criteria.
Picking requires an object height in-
crease exceeding 7 cm. Pushing requires planar placement
within 3 cm per coordinate and orientation error below 9◦.
Stacking requires alignment within (2.5, 2.5, 1.2) cm of the
target pose and open grippers. Lifting requires height above
0.9 m, a rise of at least 3 cm, and a valid two-finger grasp by
at least one arm for 15 consecutive physics steps (Sec. E).

5.1. Experimental Setup
Training and evaluation.
The tokenizer is pretrained on
40 million morphology–state pairs for 300 epochs using
eight GPUs and a global batch of 32,768. All downstream
URDFs are excluded. Policy training updates the VLM and
action head with global batch 128 while keeping the tok-
enizer frozen.
RoboTwin 2.0 uses 3,750 demonstrations
(15 tasks, 5 embodiments, 50 demonstrations each) for 30k
steps. EmbodiBench uses 15,000 for 60k steps. Full set-
tings appear in Sec. C. RoboTwin 2.0 evaluates 20 trials
per task–embodiment pair. EmbodiBench follows Sec. 4.
Both benchmarks use 1,500 trials per method with equal
task quotas, so macro-averaged and pooled success agree
up to rounding.
Comparisons.
StarVLA (joint) predicts continuous joint
actions. 256-bin uses uniform bins, MaskGIT, and ten-node
fitting.
FAST [16] uses autoregressive codes without fit-
ting. Angle+M removes the link-pose prediction head and
its reconstruction loss.
Angle also removes morphology
conditioning and graph biases and changes commitment
weight from 0.1 to 0.25. StarVLA (EE) is a separate control-
interface reference. Ours denotes the policy using the full
RoboMorpher tokenizer.
5.2. Multi-Embodiment Task Performance
RoboMorpher achieves the highest mean success among the
evaluated joint-space methods: 56.40% on RoboTwin 2.0
and 23.27% on EmbodiBench (Tabs. 2 and 3), exceeding
StarVLA (joint) by 9.73 and 15.34 percentage points (pp).
Gains across RoboTwin 2.0 tasks.
RoboMorpher im-
proves over continuous joint control on 12 of 15 tasks. The
largest gains are on bowl stacking (54% vs. 25%), micro-
phone handover (75% vs. 47%), and hammering (34% vs.
10%). Success decreases by 3–4 pp on bell clicking, dual-
bottle picking, and bottle disposal. Against 256-bin and
FAST, the mean gains are 4.80 and 5.67 pp, with improve-
ments on nine and eleven tasks, respectively.
Lifting gains on EmbodiBench.
The three lifting tasks
average 55.11% success,
compared with 14.00% for
StarVLA (joint), accounting for about 80% of the overall
gain. Picking improves from 7.11% to 12.89% on average,
and pushing from 16.00% to 28.67%. The policy improves
on six of seven non-stacking tasks, but single-arm picking
falls from 9.33% to 4.00%. All three stacking rates remain
zero, and no evaluated method exceeds 1.33% on these
tasks. This contrasts with 54% success on RoboTwin 2.0
bowl stacking, but differences in objects and success predi-
cates preclude attributing the gap solely to morphology.
Opposite-side trials yield higher lifting (61.33% vs.
52.67%) and picking (23.33% vs. 11.33%) success.
Be-
cause placement and scene geometry vary together, this
does not isolate an effect of arm placement.

RoboMorpher learns morphology-conditioned action codes

joint-space policy success on RoboTwin 2.0 and Embod-

A. Robot-Description Survey
A.1. Inventory and Scope
Table 5 lists all 33 descriptions of serial manipulators, cov-

main separate. Mass, reach, tool DoF, and sensing are tran-

hardware measurements.
Arm DoF follows the explicit survey field. Joint-type
counts may include fixed and tool joints and therefore can-
not replace that field. Likewise, mimic or other coupling
constraints can make the number of movable fingers differ
from the number of independent control dimensions. Chain
extraction and action masks use independent arm joints.
A.2. Limit and Tool Conventions
The B/M/U labels retain each entry’s stated scope, which
may cover the arm or all joints.
There are 26 bounded,
four mixed, and three unrestricted entries.
Gen2 mixes
unrestricted base/wrist with bounded shoulder/elbow joints.
Gen3 MJCF lists unrestricted base, elbow-1, wrist-1, and
wrist-3 joints.
Its URDF entry omits elbow-1 from this
list. ViperX 300 lists an unrestricted gripper joint along-

A.3. Link Records and Data Ambiguities
Table 7 preserves all 30 link records, including reported
zeros and missing entries. This table is not in one-to-one

B. MorphGen Implementation
B.1. Survey-Derived Sampling Parameters
Table 8 gives the seven Gaussian parameters computed from
Tab. 7. Each column uses its non-missing values, including
zeros, to estimate the mean and sample standard deviation.
These are linkwise sampling priors, not workspace statis-
tics.
For each arm, the generator samples an integer joint
count uniformly from the configured inclusive interval,
whose default is [6, 7]. Each provisional length is drawn
from N(µi, σ2
i ) truncated below at zero and above at
max(u, µi + 0.1) meters. The nominal upper bound u is
0.65 with probability 0.95 and 0.35 otherwise. The sampled
lengths are rescaled jointly using Eq. (2). Consequently,
the final lengths need not retain the pre-rescaling truncation
bounds.
The first displacement is along positive Z.
Later
displacements select one, two, or three active coordi-
nates with probabilities (0.60, 0.39, 0.01).
A single ac-
tive coordinate is selected from (x, y, z) with probabili-
ties (0.15, 0.15, 0.70). A coordinate pair is selected from
(xz, yz, xy) with probabilities (0.45, 0.45, 0.10).
Active
components are sampled from U(0, 1) and normalized to
unit length. The implementation rejects a direction within
5◦of the preceding direction’s antiparallel orientation in
the common frame. Roll and pitch initially take values in
{0, ±π/2, π}, and yaw is zero. Internal roll and pitch each
independently receive a Gaussian perturbation of standard
deviation 0.09 radians with probability 0.05. The terminal
orientation filter accepts a downward or horizontal termi-
nal local-Z direction, expressed by the updated rotation’s
Rzz ≤0 condition.
The exporter assigns arm limits [−2π, 2π], effort 100,
and velocity 5, attaches an ALOHA gripper, and writes plan-
ner and simulator configurations. Collision spheres have
radius 0.035 m and spacing 0.02 m. The 96 examples in
Fig. 1(b) illustrate variation, without identifying evaluation
URDFs or establishing hardware feasibility.

B.2. Inverse-Kinematics Home-Pose Selection
The home-pose stage constructs a tabletop target grid with
x ∈[0.20, 0.38], y ∈[−0.25, 0.25], and z ∈[0.18, 0.51]
meters, at spacings 0.03, 0.05, and 0.03 m, respectively. Po-
sitions receive independent Gaussian noise with standard
deviation 0.01 m. Targets are shuffled and tested in batches
of 50. cuRobo uses 32 IK seeds and self-collision check-
ing. The first batch containing a successful target provides
a randomly selected successful solution. A description is
rejected if all candidates fail.
Each joint origin rotation absorbs Rai(qhome
i
) to set the
controller zero. Unlike the axis-frame change in Eq. (1),
this changes the numerical configuration coordinates. Be-
cause limits are not shifted, the original bounded configu-
ration set need not be preserved. The IK filter checks sam-
pled targets under the collision model. It does not guarantee
collision-free motion throughout the joint space.
B.3. Feature Extraction and Frames
The feature extractor receives an explicit joint order.
It
composes fixed paths between consecutive selected mov-
able joints, rejects skipped movable joints and selected
mimic/prismatic joints, and expresses each selected rota-
tional axis as local Z. The default representation is relative
to the arm root. An optional setting can incorporate the root-
to-arm home transform into the first origin. Each valid joint
supplies translation and roll–pitch–yaw, plus its raw joint
angle. A Boolean mask separates real joints from padding
up to seven positions.

C. Tokenizer Training and Reconstruction
C.1. Network, Quantization, and Normalization
The morphology and angle branches each have two Trans-
former layers, followed by six conditional encoder and four
decoder layers (width 1024, eight heads). RVQ uses dimen-
sion 256 and two separate 2048-entry codebooks shared
across joints.
It uses EMA decay 0.8, 100 k-means ini-
tialization iterations, commitment weight 0.1, dead-code
fraction 0.1, and straight-through gradients.
Rotation-
trick gradients, entropy regularization, and autoencoder-
only warmup are disabled.
Conditional stacks use joint-
wise AdaLN-Zero. Spatial and directional chain-bias tables
start at zero. Table 9 consolidates training data and opti-
mization settings for all stages.
Min–max statistics are computed per channel over valid
training joints and shared across positions and robots. Fea-
tures map to [−1, 1], with 10−6 added to the denominator.
Padding is excluded. Raw angle radians are not wrapped.
Decoder rotations are denormalized before conversion to
matrices. Equation (10) uses normalized angle/position er-
rors and physical rotation geodesics divided by π.
The lazy loader reserves every 1000th global row for vali-
dation (fraction 0.001). This splits samples rather than mor-
phology identities. The 40M count refers to morphology–
state pairs. Unique pretraining URDF counts and sampling
densities require the data manifest. Tokenizer training com-
pletes 300 epochs (365,700 optimization steps). Training
uses eight GPUs, batch 4096 per GPU, and no gradient
accumulation (global batch 32,768).
All six joint-space
policies and the EE reference complete 30,000 steps on
RoboTwin 2.0 and 60,000 on EmbodiBench, with global
batch 128.
C.2. Ablation Configuration Differences
Angle+M retains the joint-code layout, URDF branch, and
graph biases but removes link-geometry prediction and su-
pervision.
Angle additionally removes the morphology
branch, all three graph-bias types, and policy URDF condi-
tioning. It also changes the commitment weight from 0.1 to
0.25. Thus Angle versus Angle+M changes several factors,
while the full model versus Angle+M compares link-pose
supervision under morphology conditioning. The code la-
bels this output and loss “EE,” although they cover all valid
links.
C.3. Physical Reconstruction Metrics
For each valid joint, angle error is the absolute decoder-to-
target difference in degrees without periodic wrapping. Po-
sition error is the Euclidean distance in meters between the
decoder’s direct position output and FK of the original joint
angles. Orientation error is the SO(3) geodesic angle in de-
grees between the decoded 6D rotation and the correspond-
ing FK rotation. These metrics cover all valid child-link
frames, not just the end effector. They do not evaluate FK
of reconstructed angles.
Mean, P90, and maximum are computed after pooling all
valid raw errors within each description group. This weights
individual joint/link samples equally, without balancing tra-
jectories, tasks, or morphologies. Longer trajectories and
arms with more valid joints therefore contribute more sam-
ples.
Policy success instead averages tasks equally, so
the two metrics use different weighting schemes. Padding,
missing arms, and grippers are excluded. Shardwise or join-
twise percentiles are not averaged. The encoder receives
joint angles and morphology, without link-pose input. Re-
construction excludes VLA code prediction and continuous
refinement.

totaling 3,750, consistent with the policy-training count. Ta-
ble 10 summarizes trajectory coverage.
EmbodiBench reconstruction uses the policy-training
demonstrations from Sec. 4, with 1,500 trajectories per task
and 15,000 in total.

D. VLA Training and Sampling Details
D.1. Observations, Action Layout, and Context
Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct receives 224×224 head/wrist RGB
views and an instruction containing 256-bin current-state to-
kens. The action head also receives state directly. Each arm
has eight slots, comprising seven arm joints and one gripper
channel. Masks suppress absent joints and arms. Prediction
and execution both use 50-frame chunks of absolute joint
positions and gripper commands, passed to the simulator’s
controllers.
Ten fitted joint nodes per chunk (Eq. (11)), two IDs per
joint, and one ID per gripper yield 10(2J + 1) IDs per arm,
or at most 300 for two seven-joint arms. Grippers use ordi-
nary temporal resampling and 256 bins. The 256-bin joint
baseline uses the same ridge fitting but clips fitted nodes to
its fixed quantization interval. FAST uses variable-length
autoregressive codes without node fitting. The pose head re-
ceives auxiliary geometric supervision during tokenizer pre-
training. During control, the policy refines decoded joint
actions and does not execute the predicted link poses.
LayerNorm and linear projection map each VLM layer’s
features to width 1024. The action head uses 16 heads, 32
future queries, position capacity 2048, dropout 0.2, and the
VLM’s layer count. Even blocks use VLM cross-attention.
Odd blocks use action self-attention. The discrete and con-
tinuous branches share their first two blocks, with separate
later blocks and no cross-branch attention. The VLM and
action head are trainable. The entire tokenizer, including its
morphology encoder, is frozen. Training uses BF16, FSDP,
and AdamW epsilon 10−8.

E. EmbodiBench Protocol Details
E.1. Tasks, Robot Schedules, and Scene Seeds
Table 3 lists the complete ten-task evaluation suite. Other
implemented environments are excluded. Suffixes SS/OS
denote same-side/opposite-side dual-arm placement. Both
arms use the same selected morphology. Collection and
evaluation follow task-indexed robot-ID schedules.
Evaluation requests 150 trials per task, five per URDF,
with fixed instructions and expert feasibility checking. The
initial scene seed is 100000(1 + s) for evaluation seed s.
Planner-infeasible scenes advance the seed stream without
consuming a trial, so success rates are conditional on plan-
ner feasibility.
Each accepted trial allows 300 executed
commands. The counter increments per take_action,
including every chunk frame, rather than per policy query
or physics step.
Default head/wrist cameras use 320×240 resolution and
45◦/37◦fields of view. The model resizes to 224 × 224.
Scene randomization is set separately in the run configura-
tion.

per pixel). Stacking frames show the released upper block.
These are visualization replays, not learned-policy rollouts

The reported results use an at-least-one-arm

object center must satisfy z > 0.9 m and rise at least 0.03 m
above its settled initial height. At every step, both fingers of

least 0.5 N effective normal force per finger, with surface
tolerance 0.002 m.

contacts from different grippers cannot be combined into a
grasp. Any failed condition resets the counter. At 250 Hz,
15 physics steps correspond to 0.06 s, not 15 policy actions.

ment, or simultaneous two-arm grasp requirement. Each
episode permits at most 300 action targets, predicted and
executed in 50-step chunks.

The implementation identifies methods by configura-
tion names.

percentage points. The tables report aggregate success rates

Tokenizer pretraining and downstream policy learning
use disjoint URDF sets.
EmbodiBench reconstruction
reuses policy-training demonstrations. It measures repre-

not policy generalization. No disjoint-morphology split is
