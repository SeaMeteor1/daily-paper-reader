Meituan-Robotics-0: A Vision-Language-Action Foundation
Model for Desktop Manipulation
Meituan Robotics Team∗
ABSTRACT
General-purpose desktop manipulation requires an embodied system that can acquire broad behavioral priors
from heterogeneous data, transfer them across robot embodiments, recover from closed-loop execution errors,
and preserve task-relevant state over extended horizons. We present Meituan-Robotics-0 (MR0), a vision-
language-action foundation model and embodied-agent system for stationary-base desktop manipulation. Its
data foundation combines 147 million vision-language samples with 15,060 hours of curated robot trajectories
spanning 12 embodiments. MR0 converts this heterogeneous experience into executable control through a
three-stage training recipe. Discrete action-token pre-training first aligns multimodal representations with robot
behavior. Continuous flow-matching mid-training then transfers these priors to fine-grained cross-embodiment
control. Real-world post-training further adapts the policy using successful demonstrations, human-gated
corrections, and outcome-labeled autonomous rollouts. We introduce Rollback, a data collection mechanism
that pairs replaced policy segments with human corrections for CFG-DAgger training. A unified end-effector
interface supports learning across heterogeneous embodiments, while asynchronous action-chunk merging
maintains smooth execution under variable inference latency. Because the low-level policy is reactive, an
event-driven agent converts visual observations into persistent task events and invokes task-level reassessment
only when task progress or feasibility changes. This separation preserves continuous motor control while
enabling cross-task retrieval and conditional task resumption. Evaluated as a standalone policy, MR0 achieves
93.6% success rate on RoboTwin 2.0 and 15.73% success rate on RoboDojo. Under matched HG-DAgger
post-training, MR0 achieves higher average success rate and progress score than π0.5 across the three tasks. On
Ethernet insertion, CFG-DAgger trained with Rollback data reaches 70% success and a progress score of 0.90
without value learning or advantage labeling. Qualitative agent deployments further demonstrate cross-task
event retrieval and recovery from temporarily unsatisfied task conditions. These results show that aligned
heterogeneous data, real-world post-training, and event-level memory provide complementary foundations for
transferable and long-horizon desktop manipulation.

Introduction
Vision–language–action [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7] models provide a general interface for mapping visual observations and
language instructions to robot actions. Their increasing data and model scales have substantially broadened the
range of manipulation behaviors that can be learned within a single policy. Deploying such policies on desktop
manipulation, however, exposes several limitations that cannot be addressed by scaling the action model alone.
Robot datasets [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14] collected from different sources use inconsistent observation formats, action
spaces, embodiments, and task descriptions, which prevents them from providing coherent supervision without careful
alignment. Even after data alignment, policies trained primarily on successful demonstrations receive little guidance for
recovering from the error states induced by their own closed-loop behavior. Moreover, a reactive policy conditioned
only on the current observation cannot preserve task information once it leaves the observation window. A practical
desktop-manipulation system must therefore connect aligned heterogeneous data, robust real-world policy learning, and
persistent task-level memory.
We present Meituan-Robotics-0 (MR0), a desktop-manipulation framework built around a large-scale data foundation,
a generalist VLA policy, and an event-driven embodied agent, as summarized in Figure 1. The data foundation combines
147 million vision–language samples with 15,060 hours of curated robot trajectories spanning 12 embodiments. The

Building on this aligned data foundation, the MR0 policy follows a three-stage training recipe that separates the
acquisition of broad action priors from continuous-control learning and real-world adaptation. A shared multimodal
backbone is first paired with a discrete action head and pre-trained jointly on vision-language data and tokenized
robot actions. Continuous mid-training then introduces an MMDiT [15] action head and optimizes flow matching over
a unified end-effector representation, transferring the pre-trained features to fine-grained control across single-arm
and bimanual embodiments. Real-world post-training [16, 17, 18] starts with supervised finetuning on successful
demonstrations. HG-DAgger [19] adds human corrections from policy-induced error states, while RECAP (RL with
Experience and Corrections via Advantage-conditioned Policies) [20] derives advantage labels from outcome-labeled
autonomous rollouts. We further introduce Rollback, a collection mechanism that returns to a historical anchor to
collect a human replacement while retaining the original policy segment. These paired segments supply direct positive
and negative labels to CFG-DAgger and remove the need for value learning and advantage labeling. At deployment,
asynchronous action-chunk merging integrates new predictions with commands already being executed, maintaining
smooth control without blocking on policy inference.
The VLA policy does not explicitly maintain task state across subtasks. MR0 therefore adds an event-driven embodied
agent [21, 22, 23] to retain relevant context, track task progress, and revise plans as conditions change. A Monitor
summarizes short visual windows into task-relevant events, and a Memory Manager consolidates these events into a
persistent record of task progress and environmental conditions. The Planner updates the active subtask only when a
new event changes task progress, reveals a failure, or alters whether the current objective is feasible. Motor execution
consequently remains with the VLA policy, while the Planner is invoked only when task-level reassessment is needed.

To equip MR0 with both broad visual-semantic understand-
ing and diverse cross-embodiment manipulation experi-
ence, we independently curate two complementary data re-
sources: 147 million vision–language samples and 15,060
hours of robot trajectories spanning 12 embodiments. The
vision–language corpus provides supervision for general
perception and embodied reasoning. Constructed through
a separate pipeline, the robot corpus combines RoboDesk,
licensed real-robot data, and open simulation data for ac-

2.1
Vision-Language Data
Visual reasoning for manipulation requires both broad per-
ceptual knowledge and embodied understanding grounded
in robot interaction. As shown in Figure 2, we organize
the 147M vision-language corpus into two complementary
subsets. General-purpose data account for 78% and provide supervision for visual question answering, spatial under-
standing, visual grounding, and counting. The remaining 22% consist of embodied data for scene captioning, task
planning, visual grounding, and affordance grounding. Affordance grounding jointly localizes the manipulated object

For the embodied VQA component, we augment the collected open-source datasets with a large-scale in-house corpus
derived from real-robot trajectories. To determine which supervision types should be prioritized in this corpus, we
conduct controlled CALVIN ABC-to-D ablations over eight candidate tasks and use the results to guide subsequent
large-scale data construction, as shown in Table 1. The candidates span three capability families: spatial localization,
action-related understanding, and semantic and task reasoning. All variants use the same StarVLA [26] configuration,
are trained on environments A, B, and C, and are evaluated on environment D. Table 1 reports success rates for
completing sequences of 1 to 5 consecutive subtasks, along with the average completed sequence length. The results
support including 2D target-affordance grounding and scene captioning as the action-related and semantic supervision
types, respectively. For spatial localization, we choose 2D grounding because it avoids costly geometric annotations and
scales more readily than 3D grounding. Taken together, the empirical results and practical annotation considerations
lead us to focus our in-house data expansion on three types: 2D target-affordance grounding, scene captioning, and 2D
grounding.
To ensure consistent and scalable data construction, we develop task-specific annotation pipelines for the selected
supervision types. For 2D grounding, we apply Set-of-Mark prompting [27]: the Segment Anything Model (SAM) [28]
proposes and marks candidate regions, and a vision-language model assigns an object identity to each region to form
grounding question–answer pairs. For 2D affordance grounding, we obtain the target bounding box and interaction
point from the key interaction frame of each action phase and propagate both annotations to earlier frames within that
phase. For scene captioning, a vision-language model conditions on the robot observation and task context to describe
task-relevant objects, spatial relations, gripper state, and execution progress.

Meituan-Robotics-0
Meituan-Robotics-0 aims to transfer broad priors learned from heterogeneous multimodal data to precise and robust
real-robot control. This transition requires the model to preserve its visual-semantic representations while adapting
its action interface and supervision to continuous cross-embodiment control and policy-induced error states. We first
describe the policy architecture in Section 3.1 and then present the three-stage training pipeline in Section 3.2: discrete
multimodal pre-training, continuous cross-embodiment mid-training, and real-world policy optimization.

Following the common VLA design [4, 33, 34], MR0 realizes this

For mid-training and all downstream stages, MR0 replaces the dis-

noisy action chunk. Joint self-attention exchanges information between the two streams, while the flow timestep modu-
lates block normalization through AdaLN. The state token remains on the observation side, providing condition-side
proprioceptive context throughout action generation.
3.2
Progressive Training
MR0 is trained progressively to separate three sources of difficulty: heterogeneous multimodal learning, continuous
cross-embodiment control, and recovery from policy-induced errors.
3.2.1
Discrete Multimodal Pre-Training
The shared token interface allows robot trajectories and vision–language samples to use the same autoregressive objective
without a separate action decoder. FAST action tokens occupy a dedicated vocabulary block whose embeddings are
initialized with the mean of the original embedding table. The remaining backbone parameters are inherited and trained
end-to-end, and all camera views share the native vision encoder and aspect-preserving smart-resize policy.
Each optimization step draws one batch from the VLA action pool and one from the vision–language pool. The joint
objective is

In this stage, we train on 128 GPUs using AdamW with cosine learning-rate decay, applying a lower learning rate to the

the episode-level goal with equal probability when both are available and otherwise use the available annotation. This
langmix strategy pairs the same actions with both fine-grained and task-level instructions.
3.2.3
Real-World Policy Optimization
We first fine-tune the mid-training checkpoint πmid on successful demonstrations to obtain an initial task policy. To
address policy-induced distribution shift [39], we collect human-in-the-loop (HIL) corrections and compare three
post-training algorithms. Human-gated DAgger [19] imitates demonstrations and corrections; RECAP [20] uses
trajectory outcomes to learn advantage-conditioned behavior; and CFG-DAgger uses positive and negative segments
supplied by Rollback. Each collection round adds to the cumulative dataset, and each policy is trained anew from πmid.
All three methods execute action chunks through the asynchronous controller in Section 4.1.
Human-gated DAgger. The policy controls the robot until an operator intervenes to correct a stall, error, or unsafe
motion. Only human-controlled segments are retained as corrective supervision. Demonstrations cover the task
procedure, while HIL corrections add recovery behavior in states encountered by the policy. Details are in Section C.1.
Rollback data collection. We introduce Rollback to pair replaced policy segments with human corrections from a
shared anchor. An operator pauses execution near failure, selects an earlier recorded state, returns the robot to it using
action history, and supplies a corrective continuation. In Figure 6, each anchor (t1 or t2) pairs a replaced segment
τ −with its human replacement τ +. Alongside demonstrations and human corrections, Rollback retains suboptimal
segments to inform value learning and guide policy updates.
RECAP policy optimization. RECAP trains its value model on both successful and failed trajectories reconstructed
from Rollback episodes. A trajectory ending with a replaced policy segment τ −is assigned a failure label. Policy
training uses only successful trajectories.2 The learned value model provides N-step advantages for assigning positive
(I = 1) or base (I = ∅) conditions to policy-training chunks. Both branches share the flow-matching loss. At inference,
action-space classifier-free guidance (CFG) combines base and positive predictions generated from the same initial
noise. Details are in Section C.2.

Embodied Manipulation System
MR0 augments the trained VLA policy with an inference-time system for continuous control and long-horizon task
execution. An asynchronous execution layer aligns variable-latency action predictions with robot control, as detailed
in Section 4.1. At a higher level, an event-driven agent retains task-relevant state beyond the policy’s current observation.
As summarized in Figure 8, motor control remains with the VLA policy, and high-level planning is activated only by
events that alter task progress or feasibility.
4.1

chunk generation with ongoing execution [40, 41, 42]. Our EVA-Client based stack [43] keeps control continuous by
decoupling the two: a background policy thread predicts a chunk from the latest observation at target rate fπ, while the
control loop consumes one buffered command at rate fc.

4.2
Event-Driven Embodied Agent
The chunk merger stabilizes local policy execution but does not preserve information across subtasks or determine
when a high-level plan should change. The embodied agent addresses these requirements through explicit task state,
persistent event memory, and event-triggered planning.
Task decomposition and execution. Given a user goal g, initial scene context s0, and skill library S, the Planner first
produces an ordered sequence of executable subtasks Q(0) = Pplan(g, s0, S). During execution, the agent maintains
the task state Xt = (qt, Rt), where qt is the active subtask and Rt is the remaining sequence. The active subtask serves
as the language instruction to the low-level policy, which generates actions as πVLA(ot, qt). It remains active until the
agent observes sufficient evidence of completion, failure, or a change in task feasibility. This state representation lets
the VLA policy execute continuously while the Planner operates only at task-level transition points.
9


Meituan-Robotics-0
Visual monitoring. The Monitor tracks local execution by mapping a short observation window Wt and the active
subtask to a structured caption Ct = M(Wt, qt). Conditioning on qt focuses the description on relevant objects, spatial
relations, interactions, and execution progress. The temporal window captures changes such as grasping, placement, or
object removal that may be ambiguous in a single frame. The Monitor does not decide whether a subtask has finished or
modify Xt; it only produces visually grounded evidence for event construction.
Event construction and memory. Monitor outputs enter a caption buffer in observation-time order. At each memory
update, Bt contains the captions that have not yet been processed, and the persistent history H<t stores ordered event
summaries together with their supporting keyframes. The Memory Manager converts the new captions into an event
and determines whether that event warrants high-level reassessment:
(Et, zt) = G(Bt, H<t, Xt, g),
zt ∈{0, 1}.
(9)
When the new evidence continues the same interaction, it is merged into the latest memory entry; a distinct state change
creates a new entry. For each event, the keyframe set Kt is obtained by uniformly sampling frames from the observation
windows associated with its captions. Memory insertion is independent of planner activation: routine observations can
be recorded with zt = 0, while subtask completion, execution failure, and changes in task preconditions set zt = 1.
This separation preserves information that may become relevant to a later instruction without repeatedly invoking the
Planner over an unchanged state.
Event-triggered planning. The Planner performs two related operations. Pplan constructs or revises a subtask sequence
from the goal, scene context, task state, and available skills. When zt = 1, Pdecide instead evaluates the current event,
its keyframes, relevant entries retrieved from H<t, the task state Xt, and the user goal g. The resulting decision may
preserve the active subtask, advance to the next one, wait for a temporarily unsatisfied precondition, or invoke Pplan to
regenerate the remaining sequence. The Planner judges progress from the observed task state rather than requiring the
complete action trajectory. When the event or its visual evidence is inconclusive, it leaves Xt unchanged.
Asynchronous coordination. The Monitor, Memory Manager, Planner, and VLA policy operate at different rates.
Multiple Monitor requests may run concurrently, so their outputs can arrive out of order; captions are therefore
committed according to observation time before event construction. Planner inference does not pause monitoring or
low-level execution, and captions produced during planning remain buffered for the next memory update. Task-state
mutations are serialized after each Planner decision, preventing concurrent decisions from modifying the same execution
state. The system consequently preserves continuous perception and control while keeping event memory and task
transitions temporally consistent.

RoboDojo.
RoboDojo [25] evaluates generalist manipulation policies on 42 bimanual simulation tasks spanning

6
Conclusion
We presented Meituan-Robotics-0, a vision-language-action foundation model and embodied system for stationary-base
desktop manipulation. MR0 combines heterogeneous data curation with a three-stage training recipe that connects
multimodal pre-training, continuous cross-embodiment control, and real-world policy optimization. Rollback collection
provides human corrections and replaced policy segments as direct supervision for CFG-DAgger, enabling policy
improvement without value learning or advantage labeling. At deployment, asynchronous action-chunk merging
supports continuous execution, while an event-driven agent maintains persistent context for task-level planning.
Simulation and real-robot experiments demonstrate competitive manipulation performance, and qualitative deployments
illustrate cross-task event retrieval and task resumption after temporary interruptions. These results support a systems
approach in which aligned data, corrective learning, and persistent memory address complementary requirements of
desktop manipulation. Future work will focus on agent and harness systems that interact more closely with VLA
policies, using execution feedback to guide planning, memory updates, and policy adaptation for reliable long-horizon
manipulation.

Real-Robot Setup and Data Preparation
Data collection and recording.
Our bimanual AgileX PiPER setup contains two human-operated leader arms and two
corresponding task-executing follower arms. The operator controls the follower arms through leader-arm teleoperation.
Each trajectory records three camera streams, the observed follower-arm configuration, and the commands sent to
the follower arms. These commands are supplied by the leader arms during human control and by the policy during
autonomous execution.
The demonstration dataset Ddemo, collected by teleoperation, contains only successful executions. The tasks and data
quantities used in our experiments are specified in Section 5.2.
Real-robot observation and action representation.
The policy receives synchronized images from three RGB
cameras: one head camera and two wrist cameras. Each camera captures images at a native resolution of 640 × 480.
The policy uses 3 camera images at each timestep, without temporal history.
We use Qwen3.5-VL’s Smart Resize during both post-training and inference. It preserves the aspect ratio and bounds
the visual token count, resizing images to approximately 352 × 256 before visual encoding.
The policy state is derived from the observed follower-arm configuration, and the action target from the command sent
to the follower arms. Both are converted to the same 20-dimensional end-effector representation. For each arm, this
representation contains a 3D Cartesian position, a 6D rotation, and a normalized gripper closure value, with 0 denoting

Static-interval trimming and training windows.
Before constructing post-training samples, we remove sustained

After trimming and source selection, trajectories are split at episode and control-source boundaries. Each training

Post-training implementation details.
Small random crops and rotations augment the head-camera view; color jitter

same training-set statistics used to unnormalize actions during deployment.

C.1
HG-DAgger Human Intervention
Let π0 be the SFT policy trained on demonstrations Ddemo. During collection round k, the robot executes autonomously
under πk−1 (gt = 0) until the operator takes control (gt = 1). The operator intervenes when execution stalls, errs, or
becomes unsafe, and returns control to the policy after recovery.
Only the human-controlled corrections τ + from successful episodes are retained (the samples with gt = 1), whether
collected through standard HIL or Rollback. Across rounds, these corrections accumulate with the demonstrations to
form the cumulative supervised dataset for training the next policy.
HG-DAgger changes data collection and sample selection but retains the flow-matching action loss. All retained SFT
and HG-DAgger samples receive equal sampling weight.

CFG-DAgger Conditional Policy Training
Data and labels.
In Figure 6, each pair (τ −, τ +) shares an anchor but may diverge afterward; no frame-wise alignment
is required. If the scene cannot be restored to an anchor, collection falls back to standard HIL and contributes only
positive data.
Unlike RECAP, CFG-DAgger uses these local segments directly, without the autonomous prefix: the positive data are
the demonstrations and the human corrections τ + (label I = 1), and the negative data are the replaced segments τ −
(label I = 0). Labels describe local segments, independent of episode outcomes.
Conditional policy training.
A shared policy πθ(at:t+H−1 | ot, ℓ, I) learns positive (I = 1), negative (I = 0), and
unconditional (I = ∅) branches using the flow-matching loss. Positive samples (demonstrations and τ +) use condition
dropout of 0.3 to train the unconditional branch while retaining the task instruction. Negative samples (τ −) always
retain their condition. Other autonomous segments, rollback/reset motions, and segments shorter than H = 50 are
excluded. Valid chunks are sampled without class rebalancing, and each round trains from πmid on the cumulative
dataset.
