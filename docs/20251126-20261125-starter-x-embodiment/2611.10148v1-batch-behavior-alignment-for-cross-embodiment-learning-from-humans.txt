ABSTRACT
Human demonstrations provide a scalable source of diverse manipulation data,
but leveraging them for robot learning remains challenging due to differences
in embodiment, viewpoint, action spaces, and temporal structure. We introduce
BATCH, a cross-embodiment learning framework that explicitly aligns human
and robot behaviors. BATCH learns self-supervised temporal representations
with a cycle-consistency objective to establish cross-embodiment correspondences
without manual annotation. We further introduce two complementary training
objectives that leverage these correspondences within an autoregressive video-
action diffusion model: behavior-aligned co-training and human-to-robot style
transfer. On real-world manipulation tasks, BATCH improves generalization to
unseen task configurations compared with training on unaligned data and prior
cross-embodiment baselines, with additional gains from the style-transfer objective.
Our results demonstrate that explicit behavior alignment enables more effective
transfer from human demonstrations to robot policies.

Human demonstrations offer a scalable way to expand the scene and behavior diversity of robot
datasets. In particular, wearable egocentric sensing devices, such as smart glasses (Engel et al., 2023),
provide a lightweight and portable solution for capturing natural human manipulation across diverse
environments, while on-device SLAM cameras enable accurate hand-pose tracking. Despite this
promise, directly leveraging human demonstrations for robot learning remains challenging due to
the substantial embodiment gap between humans and robots, including both visual appearances and
physical motions.
Prior studies have shown that directly co-training on cross-embodiment data can improve visual
robustness and policy generalization to unseen backgrounds and objects (Kareer et al., 2025; Yang
et al., 2026). Yet cross-embodiment data is not simply additional data from a different visual domain:
even when performing the same task, humans and robots can differ substantially in execution speed,
temporal structure, action spaces, and visual observations due to differences in embodiment. Human

demonstrations may also contain irrelevant segments that have no corresponding robot behavior. As
a result, naively scaling human demonstrations does not necessarily provide useful supervision for
robot behavior. Instead, models can separate the two embodiments and rely on embodiment-specific
cues, limiting the extent to which human experience transfers to robot control. Empirically, this
can lead to diminishing gains or even negative transfer as more poorly matched human data is
incorporated (Wei et al., 2025; Punamiya et al., 2026). We therefore hypothesize that effective cross-
embodiment transfer requires explicitly establishing behavioral correspondences between human
and robot trajectories, providing a learning signal for identifying task-relevant structure that should
transfer while discounting embodiment-specific variation.
We define behavior alignment as establishing temporal frame-level correspondences between
equivalent phases of task execution across embodiments, based on task-relevant states and interactions
rather than embodiment-specific appearance, viewpoint, or action representation. The key challenge
is to discover these correspondences automatically, without requiring manually annotated phase labels
or frame-level matches between human and robot trajectories. Achieving this requires features that
capture behavioral progression over time while suppressing embodiment-specific variation. Prior
non-learning approaches commonly apply dynamic time warping (DTW) over actions or static visual
features, which can be unreliable under large embodiment gaps or when visual states are ambiguous.
Instead, we learn self-supervised features with a temporal cycle-consistency objective (Dwibedi
et al., 2019; Donahue & Elhamifar, 2024), where each frame representation incorporates information
from multiple neighboring frames to capture behavioral progression rather than relying on a single
observation. We then apply subsequence DTW in this learned feature space to retrieve temporally
corresponding human and robot segments, accommodating differences in execution speed and
unmatched segments.
Another key contribution of our approach is how we leverage the aligned trajectories for cross-
embodiment learning. We instantiate our method within an autoregressive video-action diffusion
model (Ye et al., 2026) pretrained on in domain robot demonstrations, and finetune with two com-
plementary objectives over aligned human and robot trajectories. A behavior-aligned co-training ob-
jective learns visual dynamics and motions from both embodiments, while a cross-embodiment style-
transfer objective translates human observations into temporally corresponding robot-domain videos.
Together, these objectives learn from both embodiments while explicitly coupling them under matched
behavior, enabling more effective transfer from diverse human demonstrations to robot policies.
We evaluate our approach on two manipulation tasks from EgoVerse (Punamiya et al., 2026): single-
arm Object in Container, which requires continuous execution, and bimanual Cup on Saucer, which
requires precise grasping and bimanual coordination. We consider out-of-domain test scenarios
presented in the human demonstrations but absent from the robot training data. Across the two tasks,
our approach improves out-of-domain policy performance by an average of 45% over co-training
alone and 138% over the robot-only pretrained model. Removing behavior alignment substantially
degrades performance, highlighting the importance of reliable human-robot correspondences, while
the style-transfer objective provides further gains with aligned pairs. These results demonstrate
that both establishing behavioral correspondences and effectively leveraging them are critical for
cross-embodiment transfer.

RELATED WORK
Video Temporal Alignment. Video temporal alignment establishes frame-level correspondences
across videos of similar behaviors despite variations in embodiment, viewpoint, and appearance.
One foundational class of approaches is Temporal Cycle-Consistency (TCC) (Dwibedi et al., 2019;
Hadji et al., 2021), which learns a shared embedding space from unaligned videos without manual
labels by enforcing cycle-consistent nearest-neighbor matches. GTCC (Donahue & Elhamifar, 2024)
further improves the robustness of TCC through multi-neighbor, multi-cycle matching modeled
with Gaussian mixtures. In robotics, TCC-style representations have been used to derive dense
task-progress rewards for cross-embodiment learning (Zakka et al., 2022). Separately, Dynamic
Time Warping (DTW) is a common technique used to align cross-embodiment demonstrations with
different execution speeds (Punamiya et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026). Our approach
combines GTCC and DTW: GTCC learns cross-video correspondences in a shared embedding space,
while DTW produces globally consistent temporal alignments across human and robot demonstrations
while accounting for different execution speeds.

Cross-Embodiment Learning from Human Data.
Human demonstrations provide scalable
manipulation data with greater scene and behavior diversity than costly robot demonstrations, but
effective transfer remains challenging due to embodiment gap. Prior attempts include learning shared
skill representations (Xu et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2025), using transferable intermediate representations
such as object flow (Xu et al., 2025), or editing human videos into robotized demonstrations (Lepert
et al., 2025a;b; Wang et al., 2026). However, these approaches can be limited in scalability with
additional processing and tuning. More recent approaches directly co-train on human and robot
data (Kareer et al., 2025; Qiu et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025; Tao et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2026;
Punamiya et al., 2026), with follow-up works seeking to improve cross-embodiment transfer through
observation alignment (Punamiya et al., 2025), embodiment-agnostic visual prediction (Li et al.,
2026a), or image- and action-space alignment (Li et al., 2026c). Our work studies whether temporal
behavior alignment between human and robot trajectories can further support cross-embodiment
transfer while integrating naturally into existing co-training formulation.
Video Action Models jointly model future visual observations and robot actions, providing richer
supervision by learning scene dynamics alongside robot control. Early methods such as GR-1 and
GR-2 combine visual generation with action prediction (Wu et al., 2024; Cheang et al., 2024). More
recent approaches, including UVA (Li et al., 2025), UWM (Zhu et al., 2025), and VideoVLA (Shen
et al., 2025), more tightly couple video and action modeling, while Cosmos Policy (Kim et al., 2026),
DreamZero (Ye et al., 2026), LingBot-VA (Li et al., 2026b), and LingBot-VA 2.0 (Zhang et al., 2026)
scale this paradigm with large video backbones and video-action pretraining. Our work builds on this
paradigm to study cross-embodiment policy learning from behaviorally aligned human and robot
trajectories.

3.1
CROSS-EMBODIMENT BEHAVIOR ALIGNMENT
We consider human and robot demonstrations of the same manipulation task. Following EgoV-
erse (Punamiya et al., 2026), human demonstrations are collected using Aria glasses, while robot
demonstrations are collected through teleoperation (see Appendix Section A for data collection
3


details). Despite sharing the same task objective, the two datasets can differ substantially in
embodiment, background, viewpoint, and execution speed, making direct frame-level matching
unreliable. We therefore seek a representation that is robust to these cross-embodiment differences
while preserving temporal task progress. Starting from pre-extracted image features, we learn a
shared temporal embedding space using the self-supervised cycle-consistency objective of Donahue
& Elhamifar (2024), encouraging human and robot frames at corresponding stages of task execution
to be close in the learned space. These correspondences are characterized by similar object states
and interactions rather than visual appearance alone. To further account for differences in execution
speed, we recover explicit frame-level correspondences using subsequence dynamic time warping
(DTW), which finds the minimum-cost alignment in the learned embedding space while allowing
non-uniform temporal warping.

3.1.2
DATASET GENERATION WITH DTW RETRIEVAL
To construct behavior-aligned human-robot pairs, we use subsequence DTW in the learned embedding
space. Given a robot query window u1:L and a candidate human sequence v1:T , we define the
pairwise embedding cost as Cij = ∥ui −vj∥2. Subsequence DTW finds the minimum-cost human
sub-segment while allowing temporal warping to account for differences in execution speed. For each
human candidate, we select the endpoint j⋆= arg minj D[L, j], where D[L, j] is the cumulative
DTW cost of aligning the full robot query to a human sub-segment ending at j, and backtrack from
j⋆to recover the matched sub-segment. We rank all human candidates by their normalized DTW
cost and retain the top-k lowest-cost matches.
3.2
CROSS-EMBODIMENT VIDEO ACTION MODEL
Given the aligned human and robot trajectories, we train a cross-embodiment video action model
aiming to transfer task-relevant visual knowledge from human demonstrations to the robot domain.
Our model (shown in Figure 2B) builds on a video action diffusion transformer (Ye et al., 2026) that
jointly predicts future visual observations and chunks of robot actions. Training consists of two stages:
we first pretrain on robot demonstrations, then finetune on aligned human-robot trajectories with two
complementary objectives: (1) joint co-training, which processes human and robot trajectories as
attention-isolated streams with shared model parameters, and (2) human-to-robot style transfer, which
predicts robot future video from the aligned human video, explicitly encouraging human-to-robot
visual transfer. At deployment, the model predicts actions from unseen robot observations observed
only in human data.
Backbone.
Our policy builds on Wan2.1-I2V-14B (Team & Group, 2025). We retain its first 8
transformer blocks, video VAE, and CLIP ViT-H/14 image encoder (Ilharco et al., 2021). Since we
consider a single-task setting, we remove text conditioning.
Tokenization. Each training sample consists of 9 head-camera frames, a 16-step future action chunk,
the current proprioceptive state, and one conditioning frame from each wrist camera. For both human
and robot demonstrations, states and actions are expressed in the coordinate frame of the Aria glasses.
The video VAE compresses the 9 head-camera frames into three latent frames, where the first latent
frame provides conditioning context and the remaining two form a causal block to be predicted.
For each embodiment, the transformer input is organized into four token groups: (1) clean video
tokens, obtained by patchifying uncorrupted latent frames on a shared canvas by concatenating the
head and wrist latents along the width dimension; (2) noisy video tokens, obtained by patchifying the
noised head-camera latents; (3) a register consisting of 16 action tokens and one state token, projected
using embodiment-specific encoders; and (4) CLIP context tokens, obtained by encoding the first
frame from each available camera into 257 tokens per image and injected through cross-attention.
The transformer predicts flow velocities at the noisy video and action token positions, with the
corresponding prediction targets determined by the training objective. Token counts and remaining
details are given in Appendix Table 2.
Human and robot streams. For each aligned human-robot pair sampled from the dataset described
in Section 3.1.2, we construct human and robot token streams and concatenate them into a single
transformer sequence. Both streams share the same transformer weights; they differ only in their
inputs and embodiment-specific state/action encoders and decoders. The robot stream receives head
and wrist camera observations, whereas the human stream contains only the head-camera observation,
with the missing wrist-camera regions zero-padded.
As shown in Figure 2 (b), attention within each stream follows a block-causal structure. Noisy video
tokens attend to the clean visual context and to tokens within their own noisy video block. Action
tokens additionally attend to the noisy video block and the current state token. We further design the
two streams so that each can be processed independently of whether the other is present. First, rotary
positional indices are reset at the beginning of each stream, preventing the positional representation of
one embodiment from depending on the length of the other. Second, CLIP cross-attention is separated
by embodiment: robot queries attend only to robot CLIP tokens, while human queries attend only
to human CLIP tokens. As a result, concatenating the two streams does not alter the within-stream
computation, while still allowing them to share weights.

4.1
BEHAVIOR ALIGNMENT EVALUATION
We evaluate whether the learned representation supports temporal alignment between robot and
human demonstrations. Given a robot query segment, we retrieve and align the corresponding
segment from human demonstrations, and evaluate the resulting frame-level correspondences against
ground-truth phase annotations.
Tasks and evaluation protocol. We evaluate on two tasks with distinct temporal structures and
embodiment-dependent speed differences, shown in Figure 3. In Object in Container, each episode
contains 6-7 repetitions of the four-phase manipulation cycle, creating multiple valid occurrences of
the same phase within a trajectory. Human demonstrations execute the cycle approximately 2× faster
than the robot. In Cup on Saucer, each episode progresses through three phases, with the human
operating 3.4× faster than the robot. For both tasks, we evaluate against an in-domain (ID) human set
collected in the same environment as the robot demonstrations and an out-of-distribution (OOD) set
collected in different scenes. For each robot episode, we sample 400 evenly spaced query windows.
Each query is aligned against every human episode in the candidate set. We retain the minimum-cost
alignment from each human episode and average the resulting alignment metrics. Details of the
evaluation dataset can be found in the Appendix Table 2 and Section C.2.

embeddings learned with GTCC, while GTCC w/o DTW uses the same embeddings with rigid
sliding, isolating the effect of temporal warping. GTCC + rel./abs. actions concatenates each GTCC
feature with a 7-DoF end-effector pose per arm before applying DTW. We consider either absolute

Our results in Figure 4 highlight the complementary benefits of human data finetuning, behavior
alignment, and explicit cross-embodiment transfer objective. EgoVerse improves over Robot Only
on both tasks, showing that co-training with human demonstrations is beneficial. However, OURS
achieves the strongest performance on both tasks, indicating that explicitly coupling the human and
robot streams through the style-transfer objective provides additional gains beyond co-training alone.
Replacing the style-transfer objective with an OT loss in OURS w/ OT also yields strong performance,
outperforming EgoVerse on Object in Container and matching its performance on Cup on Saucer,
while still falling short of OURS. This suggests that our style-transfer objective provides a more
effective cross-embodiment learning signal than directly matching input representations with an
OT loss. Meanwhile, removing behavior alignment in OURS w/o alignment degrades performance
on both tasks. This underscores the importance of pairing human and robot trajectories based
on behavioral correspondence when applying cross-embodiment supervision. Overall, behavior
alignment and the cross-embodiment style-transfer objective provide complementary gains for
human-to-robot transfer.
Limitation. Our alignment approach assumes shared behavioral structure between human and robot
demonstrations and may struggle when strategies or action orderings differ substantially. Combining
VLM-based coarse semantic alignment with our frame-level refinement could help address this
limitation. Alignment errors may also introduce mismatched supervision into the style-transfer
objective. Finally, BATCH requires robot demonstrations during post-training with human data;
reducing this dependence remains an important direction for future work.
5
CONCLUSION
We present BATCH, a cross-embodiment learning framework that combines behavior alignment
with a cross-embodiment video-action model. We use GTCC with DTW to temporally align human
and robot behaviors in a learned embedding space, then train on the aligned demonstrations with joint
co-training and human-to-robot style transfer. Across two real-world manipulation tasks, training
with aligned data consistently outperforms unaligned training, while style transfer provides further
gains beyond co-training alone. These results demonstrate the value of explicitly establishing and
leveraging behavior correspondences for effective human-to-robot transfer.

BEHAVIOR ALIGNMENT DETAILS
Human-robot pairs are computed once per task before policy training. Every frame of the robot and
human recordings is embedded with the GTCC encoder described in Section 3.1. Given a robot query
window u1:L and a candidate human sequence v1:T , we define the pairwise embedding cost as
Cij = ∥ui −vj∥2.
We apply subsequence dynamic time warping (DTW) to find the minimum-cost human subsequence
that aligns to the full robot query while allowing non-uniform temporal warping. Let D[i, j] denote
the cumulative DTW cost. We select the endpoint
j⋆= arg min
j
D[L, j],
and backtrack from (L, j⋆) to recover the matched human subsequence and frame-level warping path.
For each robot window, we rank human candidates by normalized DTW cost and store the top-k
matches together with their warping paths in raw frame units (k = 5 for Object in Container and
k = 1 for Cup on Saucer). At each training step, one candidate is sampled uniformly, and the
corresponding human frames, states, and actions are read at the indices specified by the warping path,
effectively time-warping the human trajectory onto the robot window.
Computational cost. Let N and M denote the numbers of robot and human episodes, respectively,
and T the episode length in frames. Each GTCC training epoch pairs every episode in the larger
dataset with one from the other embodiment, yielding a cost of O(max(N, M) T 2), linear in the
larger dataset size.
Retrieval costs O((N + M) T + NMLT 2): embedding all episodes once takes O((N + M) T),
while matching O(NT) robot query windows of length L against M human episodes via subsequence
DTW takes O(NMLT 2). These matches are independent and readily parallelizable.
For N = M = 30 and T ≈200, training takes about 100 s per epoch on one GPU, or 5.5 GPU-hours
for 200 epochs. On the scale of our datasets shown in Table 2, retrieval takes a few minutes for both
embedding and subsequence DTW matching on a single CPU core.

B.2.2
ATTENTION STRUCTURE
Each embodiment is represented as a separate stream consisting of clean conditioning tokens, noisy
future-video tokens, state tokens, and action tokens. Within each stream, we use block-causal self-
attention over the conditioning context and the predicted block. Noisy video tokens attend to the
stream’s clean conditioning context and to the noisy video tokens within the same block. Action
tokens additionally attend to the corresponding noisy video block, state token, and action tokens.
Positional information is also kept stream-specific: rotary position indices for both image tokens and
register tokens (e.g., state, action tokens) restart from zero for each embodiment. Cross-attention to
the CLIP image features of the conditioning frame is likewise separated by embodiment, such that
each stream attends only to its own conditioning image features.
The two fine-tune objectives differ only in how clean-context attention is shared across the human
and robot streams:
• Joint co-training. Human and robot streams are attention-isolated. Each stream attends only to its
own clean conditioning context, with separate rotary positions and CLIP conditioning. As a result,
the robot predictions are independent of the presence of the human stream, while both streams are
optimized jointly within the same model.
• Style transfer. The human stream is placed before the robot stream, and the robot stream attends
to the clean human video context together with its robot CLIP conditioning; the robot conditioning
latent and registers are masked.

INFERENCE
At deployment the sequence contains only the robot stream: the current head and wrist images are
encoded once into a key-value cache as the clean conditioning frame, and the policy denoises one
video block together with the 16 action tokens, conditioned on the current state.
We sample with a 16-step UniPC multistep flow solver (shift 5). The transformer is evaluated on 8 of
the 16 steps under a fixed schedule and the remaining steps reuse the most recent velocity prediction.
The action schedule runs to σ = 0, while the video schedule is rescaled to end at σ = 0.25. We
execute the first 8 of the 16 predicted actions at 10 Hz, then reset the cache and replan from a new
observation. The inference latency is 0.35s for single arm policy and 0.42s for bimanual policy on
one GeForce RTX 5090 GPU.

D.1
PER-SCENARIO PERFORMANCE
We conduct 5 one-minute rollouts per scenario for Object in Container and 10 rollouts per scenario
for Cup on Saucer. For Cup on Saucer, T1 tests object generalization using only unseen object
sets (red and blue), balanced across left- and right-side cup placements. T2 introduces a white
tablecloth and mixes unseen object sets (e.g., red and blue) with seen object sets (e.g., light blue and
white), while evenly varying the cup side across rollouts. T3 uses the same four object sets on a gray
tablecloth, with 5 rollouts using seen objects and 5 using unseen objects, and an even split between
left- and right-side cup placements.
Figure 7 breaks down policy performance across individual evaluation scenarios. Across all scenarios
on both tasks, OURS achieves the strongest performance, with especially large gains over EgoVerse
under more background distribution shifts (e.g., T1, T4 in Object in Container and T2, T3 in Cup
on Saucer). The consistent drop for OURS w/o alignment further shows that behavior alignment is
important for effectively transferring information from human demonstrations.
