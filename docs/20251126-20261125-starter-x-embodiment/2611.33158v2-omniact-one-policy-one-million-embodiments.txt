Abstract
A single policy that controls diverse robots must not only
transfer task knowledge across embodiments, but also adapt
its behavior to each robot’s capabilities.
Existing ap-
proaches facilitate transfer through shared action represen-
tations, but these representations do not necessarily cap-
ture how differences in robot morphology should shape
manipulation strategies. We introduce OMNIACT, a cross-
embodiment manipulation policy that conditions directly
on each robot’s kinematics in a specific scene to produce
embodiment-specific behavior. This kinematic graph repre-
sents the robot’s joint positions, motion axes, and connectiv-
ity to its visual observation, allowing the policy to account
for how its embodiment relates to objects and surfaces in the
scene. A flow-matching policy predicts relative joint motions
for each node, enabling control across robots with different
degrees of freedom and kinematic configurations. To learn

1. Introduction
A single policy capable of controlling robots with diverse
embodiments must solve two related problems: transfer-
ring task knowledge across robots and adapting behavior
to each robot’s physical capabilities. Existing approaches
have made substantial progress on the former by identify-
ing representations and interfaces that can be shared across
embodiments. Generalist vision-language-action models use
shared end-effector commands, unified action tokenizers,
or learned action latents [4, 9, 21, 27, 36]. Object-centric
methods represent desired object motions or interactions that
can be transferred between robots [3, 18, 25, 44]. These
approaches facilitate transfer by exploiting commonalities
across embodiments. However, transferring task knowledge
does not necessarily lead to effective control of each robot.
Two robots pursuing the same goal may require different
grasps, trajectories, or contact strategies because of differ-
ences in their kinematics, workspace, and dexterity.
Learning embodiment-specific behaviors presents several
challenges. First, a policy must reason about the robot’s
geometry in relation to the observed scene. The usefulness
of a joint configuration depends on where its joints and mo-
tion axes lie relative to the objects and obstacles it must
interact with. Existing policies incorporate descriptions such
as URDFs or kinematic graphs, but often encode the robot
independently of the scene [7, 32, 35]. Second, the policy
must accommodate different kinematic structures, includ-
ing variations in joint types, degrees of freedom, and link
topology, while still supporting a common control interface.
Existing approaches have demonstrated transfer across rela-
tively small collections of manipulators [28, 39, 47]. Third,
the policy must learn how changes in morphology affect
behavior so that it can control embodiments not encountered
during training. This requires experience across a sufficiently
diverse set of physical structures. Prior efforts to scale learn-
ing across hundreds of morphologies have primarily focused
on locomotion [1, 15] and not manipulation.
We introduce OMNIACT, a policy trained across one
million embodiments, capable of adapting behavior to each
embodiment and what it can achieve in a given scene. Our
approach represents each robot through a scene-grounded
kinematic graph, expressing its joint geometry in the 3D
coordinate frame of each observing camera. Each joint and
fingertip is represented as a node containing its camera-frame
position, motion axis, joint type, proprioceptive state, and
kinematic connectivity. This allows the policy to relate the
robot’s physical structure to the corresponding visual obser-
vations. We pair this representation with a variable-DoF
action representation that jointly predicts relative joint mo-
tions and end-effector pose updates using a flow-matching
policy. By unifying the indexing scheme across all embod-
iments, our model learn to support different numbers and
configurations of joints within the same network. These two
components allow the policy to generate motions directly
from each robot’s kinematic structure and its relationship to
the scene.
Training a policy to exploit these differences requires sub-
stantial diversity in robot morphology. A policy trained on a
handful of embodiments may learn effective manipulation
strategies for those robots without learning how to adapt
them to new ones. We develop a procedure to instantiate
one million unique robot embodiments and create algo-
rithms to automatically generate trajectories for each of
them in simulation. Our data generation pipeline varies joint
configurations, link geometry, and kinematic topology to
generate embodiments; we use inverse-kinematics screening
and physics-based acceptance to ensure feasibility of each
trajectory. The resulting training distribution exposes the
policy to a broad range of robot configurations and allow us
to test on unseen embodiments.
We evaluate OMNIACT on embodiments and scenes held
out from training. On OMNIACT-BENCH, it succeeds in
63.0% of episodes within 500 steps, against 57.9% for the
strongest baseline and 17.8% for π0.5; on the task families
GPT-6 Astra was evaluated on, it reaches 64.7% against
GPT-6 Astra’s 41.1% at the same step budget and 54.8%
with twice the budget (Table 3). On a real OpenYAM arm
with a fine-tip gripper, neither seen in training, OMNIACT
completes 13 of 20 pick-and-place trials. The only baseline
to succeed at all is MolmoAct2-BimanualYAM, for which
this arm is in distribution, at 7 of 20; every other baseline
completes none (Table 5). We observe that on this arm the
policy sometimes makes use of the long final link specific to
this unseen embodiment, extending it horizontally and then
rotating the gripper to reach far targets (Section 3.4), a behav-
ior not seen from MolmoAct2 trained exclusively on YAM.
These results suggest that policies grounded in a robot’s
kinematics are a path towards better cross-embodiment gen-
eralization.

2. Method
We introduce OMNIACT, a single policy that learns cross-
embodiment manipulation across robots with different kine-
matic structures. Our approach has three components. First,
we represent each robot through a scene-grounded kinematic
graph that expresses its geometry in the coordinate frame
of its visual observations. Second, we develop a variable-
DoF action representation that jointly predicts joint motions
and end-effector poses across different robot configurations.
Third, we generate diverse embodiments and collect success-
ful manipulation trajectories in simulation to train the policy
across one million robots.
Policy formulation.
We consider manipulation tasks
across robots with different numbers of joints, link geome-
tries, and kinematic structures. At each time step t, the policy
receives a language instruction ℓ, multi-view RGB observa-
tions It = {It,v}V
v=1, and the robot’s current configuration
and proprioceptive state. The robot’s embodiment specifies
its kinematic topology, joint types, limits, and geometric
parameters.
The policy predicts an action chunk containing relative
joint motions and end-effector (EE) pose updates. Both pre-
dictions are supervised during training and used for control
during execution. A prefix of the predicted chunk is executed
before obtaining updated observations and replanning. The
same policy parameters are shared across all embodiments.
We encode RGB observations using SigLIP v2 [38] and
language instructions using Qwen 2.5 [2]. Robot geometry,
configuration, and proprioception are represented through
kinematic node tokens.
2.1. Scene-Grounded Kinematic Graph
The motions available to a robot depend on its kinematic
structure and how that structure is situated relative to the
visual observation.
Kinematic structure.
We decompose each robot into
nodes representing arm joints, gripper drivers, grip-
per tips, and annotated points such as the tool cen-
ter point (TCP). Each node n has a type τn
∈
{terminal, revolute, prismatic, point} and an actuation in-

and annotated points. The kinematic topology is encoded
through shortest-path distances between nodes, which enter
the Transformer as attention biases [43]. Nodes are ordered
according to their kinematic structure and left-padded to a
maximum supported node count, keeping end-effector nodes
at the final positions of the sequence. This provides a con-
sistent indexing scheme across different embodiments while
preserving their variable numbers of joints.
Joint proprioception.
Joint states differ in their physical
units, limits, and ranges across embodiments. We center
each joint coordinate around the midpoint of its limits and
convert it to a common numerical scale: pn = κn(qn −cn),
where qn is the current joint position, cn is the midpoint of
its limit range, and κn is a joint-type-dependent unit factor.
Revolute joints are expressed in radians and prismatic joints
in decimeters. The centered scalar is expanded into Fourier
features and encoded through an MLP.

2.2. Variable-DoF Action Prediction
Robots differ in their numbers of joints, joint types, and
available motion directions. We use a node-aligned action
representation in which the output follows the same padded
ordering as the input kinematic graph. This allows a single
network to predict actions for different embodiments while
maintaining correspondence between each node’s geometry
and its control signals.
Each output node is processed by two prediction heads:
a relative joint-motion head and a relative pose head. The
joint-motion head predicts changes in the robot’s joint co-
ordinates, while the pose head predicts relative EE poses
associated with the corresponding end-effector nodes. Both
heads are trained through flow matching, and both predic-
tions contribute to the control executed by the robot.
We normalize joint actions using empirical statistics as-
sociated with their left-padded node slots, which we find
effective for representing diverse embodiments. Action out-
puts are masked according to the valid nodes and action
quantities for each robot.

2.3. Scaling Embodiments
Embodiment generation.
A policy trained on a small set
of robots may learn manipulation strategies specific to those
kinematics, limiting its ability to adapt to unfamiliar em-
bodiments. We therefore procedurally generate one million
robot embodiments spanning different kinematic structures,
joint configurations, and link geometries. Each embodiment
is generated deterministically from a random seed, gener-
ating its morphology, joint limits, actuator dynamics, link
geometry, inertia, and appearance. This makes each robot
reproducible without separately storing its complete descrip-
tion.
We filter generated candidates for self-collision and grasp
feasibility. Grasp feasibility is evaluated by solving inverse
kinematics for representative target grasp poses on randomly
sampled objects. 84.4% of all generated embodiments fail
self-collision and 97.5% of the surviving embodiments fail
grasp checks despite being sampled from distributions de-
signed to produce realistic robots. 6-DoF, 7-DoF, and 8-
DoF embodiments have pass rates of 0.016%, 0.299%, and
0.653% overall pass rate. Filtering these candidates avoids
expensive trajectory collection for embodiments that cannot
perform simple manipulation tasks.
Generating trajectories per embodiment.
We collect
manipulation trajectories in MolmoSpaces [22], instantiating
each feasible embodiment in randomly generated scenes
with different tasks and initial configurations. We plan two
scene-task instances per embodiment.
Trajectory generation uses a planner that proposes lo-
cally feasible motions under the current robot’s kinematic
constraints. At each step, the planner considers feasible
immediate motions towards the next desired pose [22] with-
out explicitly planning an embodiment-specific long-horizon
strategy. Different from MolmoSpaces’ use of top-down
poses calculated assuming the Franka robot, we randomly
rotate the grasp poses and allow for any kinematically fea-
sible pose on the robot to form the support. We filter for
successful episodes, producing demonstrations of manip-
ulation behaviors that are feasible for each robot. 6-, 7-,
and 8-DoF embodiments have flat episode success rates of
35.9%, 34.8%, and 36.4%, compared to embodiment screen-
ing’s DoF-dependent pass rates. Successful episodes are
stored with sufficient simulation state to reconstruct the en-
vironment at intermediate steps. Table 1 summarizes the

3. Experiments
The key takeaways are as follows: (a) In simulation on
robots seen to baselines, OMNIACT performs better or on
par with existing methods despite not seeing the robot itself;
(b) In simulation on unseen robots, OMNIACT outperforms
all baseline methods, including embodiment-specific and
embodiment-agnostic policies, as well as the generalist VLM
GPT-6 Astra, despite a smaller step budget; (c) In real-world
deployment, OMNIACT performs better or on par with ex-
isting methods on robots these existing methods specialize
towards; (d) OMNIACT adapts its motion strategy based on
the embodiment and the task at hand, instead of retargeting
one canonical grasp across bodies.
Training data. We train on our corpus of 1M procedurally
generated manipulators (Section 2.3) performing manipula-
tion in MolmoSpaces scenes [22].
Baselines. We compare with frontier policies including
π0.5 [19], DreamZero [42], MolmoAct2 [13], GPT-6 Astra
(max thinking), and embodiment generalization specialists
UMI [8] and UMI-on-Air [16]. To address the possibility
that a multi-view setup would provide better visual context
compared to UMI’s single-view setup, we add a variant of
UMI supplied with a 3-view camera setup as another base-
line. We train UMI on the same simulated trajectories as
our training but with UMI’s setup, to measure the effects
of our architectural choices that facilitate our specific in-
put conditioning (Section 2.1). In real-world deployment,
we additionally measure LAP-3B [45], a zero-shot cross-
embodiment transfer model, at their released checkpoint.
Benchmarks. We evaluate policies in three settings: i) on
the existing MolmoSpaces benchmark that uses a robot that
is in-distribution for the baseline policies, ii) on OMNIACT-
BENCH, our benchmark based on held-out training data so
unseen to all policies, and iii) on real-world tasks.
Implementation details. OMNIACT is a 400M-parameter
flow-matching policy over a fixed 12-slot node and action
layout. It observes RGB views encoded by a frozen SigLIP 2
image encoder [38], language encoded by the frozen text
backbone of Qwen2.5-VL [2], and the per-node embodiment
representation of Section 2.1. Training samples are 32-frame
windows with a 31-step action prediction horizon. We train
with an effective batch size of 128 with a learning rate of
10−3 for 520K steps. At evaluation the policy predicts a
31-step chunk and executes the first 8 steps every chunk.
3.1. Evaluation on seen robots in simulation
We evaluate OMNIACT in simulation on the MolmoSpaces
benchmark, which uses the Franka Panda in the DROID
configuration. All baselines have seen the Franka robot
during training, whereas OMNIACT does not.
OMNIACT performs on par with embodiment-specialized
models on their expert embodiments. Table 2(full scenes)
reports success on 2,622 held-out episodes under the same
500-step cap. OMNIACT reaches 69.1% overall, against
63.0% for MolmoBot and under 30% for the DROID-trained
policies, and leads on four of the six task families, with
the largest margins where the task constrains placement
or approach: for example, +15.5 percentage points over
MolmoBot on placing next to a reference object.

and prompt format, and handles changes in camera and
robot pose about as well as baselines. π0.5 and MolmoAct2

3.2. Evaluation on unseen robots in simulation
Next we want to test how well baseline methods generalize to
novel embodiments. We use the held-out OMNIACT-BENCH
benchmark (Section 2.3).
OMNIACT generalizes to unseen embodiments in simula-
tion. Rollout success on a random subset of 2000 episodes
sampled from our benchmark is reported in Table 3. From
the results, we can see that OMNIACT outperforms base-
lines at the same number of rollout steps, leading on six
task families, and its overall success rate of 63.0% is 5.1
percentage points above the next best method, UMI with all
camera views. Beyond policies, the general-purpose GPT-6
Astra model also struggles with random embodiments, trail-
ing OMNIACT by 23.6 percentage points at the same step
count on the task families both were evaluated on (41.1% vs.
64.7%). We empirically observe that GPT-6 Astra, thanks
to its language and multimodal reasoning capabilities, per-
sistently retries with different solutions; however, even at
double the step count, it is still behind OMNIACT by 9.9
percentage points.

3.4. Motion strategy analysis
During evaluation, we notice that when the object pickup
site is close to the receptacle, OMNIACT sometimes takes
advantage of OpenYAM’s long link leading up to the grip-
per, simply rotating the gripper in the Z axis to reach the
receptacle instead of lifting and dropping.
This is plausibly a side-effect of our data generation
method: we allow for poses at different phases to have dif-
ferent orientations; thus, when the receptacle is close by,
rotating is a viable, if not more direct, strategy. Because ev-
ery trajectory in our corpus is one that its own embodiment
could execute, the data does not commit to a single grasp
retargeted to different embodiments: depending on the body
and the object, successful grasps approach from above, from
the side, or in between. We examine whether OMNIACT
carries the flexibility to change its strategy depending on the
embodiment and the task.
7


Setup. We perform this experiment on OpenYAM, a normal-
sized arm, and Lite6, an arm relatively limited in reach. A
knife, a cube, and a bowl are visible. The knife lies on the
surface and cannot be picked up easily from the top.
On real tasks, OMNIACT adapts its motion strategy to the
task. Figure 5(a) shows two grasps by the same OpenYAM
arm from the same deployment. For the cube (top), OMNI-
ACT approaches from above and closes the gripper vertically.
For the knife (bottom), it instead pitches the wrist until the
gripper is level with the table and closes on the blade from
the side, the orientation in which the thin body fits between
the fingertips with maximum contact area. The policy treats
the wrist as a free degree of freedom and picks the grasp
orientation the object calls for, rather than holding a fixed
top-down pose.
On real robots, OMNIACT adapts its motion strategy to
the embodiment. Figure 5(b) fixes the task and varies the
body. When the object is placed close to the robot, both
OpenYAM and Lite6 approaches normally from the top.
However, when the object is placed at the edge of Lite6’s
relatively short reach (bottom), it approaches from the side
with the gripper lying about 20◦from the table, while on
OpenYAM (top) it approaches from the top as OpenYAM can
easily reach the same distance. Additionally, when attempt-
ing to pick from the side, the angles of approach are slightly
different between OpenYAM and Lite6 (Figure 5(a)(b) bot-
tom), such that both robots would be lying as flat as possible
without putting excessive force on the table, which would
trigger a gripper collision in simulation and a collision stop
in real drivers.
Overall, we can see that OMNIACT adapts its motion strat-
egy to the embodiment and the task at hand, rather than
retargeting one canonical grasp across bodies and forced
into an infeasible pose on one of them.
3.5. Ablation study
Scaling training embodiments helps.
We subsample the
generated training embodiments to one half and one eighth
of the corpus (400K and 100K, respectively), holding archi-
tecture and training schedule fixed (Table 6 & Figure 6). Suc-
cess falls monotonically: −6.5% at one half, and −26.8%
at one eighth, on a 500 rollout step limit.
Scene-grounded kinematic graph and camera-view in-
puts are important to success. We then remove inputs
at fixed data (Table 6). Dropping the per-node kinematic
graph tokens costs 5.1 percentage success: the body is
partially inferable from pixels and proprioception, but not
fully, demonstrating the advantage of supplying the scene-
grounded graph through camera-grounded nodes instead of
through robot-frame proprioception. Removing the exocen-
tric views as well, leaving only wrist observations, costs a
further 36.8%.

4. Discussion
We find that scaling diverse robotic embodiments and sup-
plying scene-grounded kinematic graphs lead to better gen-
eralization to unseen robots on real-world tasks, enabling
8


body-specific motion strategies for the task at hand. Scal-
ing the number of training embodiments drives performance
on held-out simulated embodiments. The scene-grounded
kinematic graph provides a small amount of improvement
as well, but farther away from the training distribution, the
graph’s contribution appears much larger (e.g. OpenYAM).
These results suggest that embodiment scale covers bodies
that resemble the training ones, while the graph carries the
policy to a body at the edge of that distribution.
An open question is to scale embodiments types further,
including bimanual arms, humanoids, and everyday objects
such as tools, equipment, and vehicles.
