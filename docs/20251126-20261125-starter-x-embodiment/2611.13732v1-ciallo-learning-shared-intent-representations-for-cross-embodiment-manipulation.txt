Abstract
Robot
demonstrations
contain
reusable
manipulation
knowledge, but transferring this knowledge across embod-
iments requires representations that bridge differences in
control interfaces.
Action reconstruction alone does not
ensure that similar behaviors across robots share a rep-
resentation. To address this gap, we introduce CIALLO,
an action codec that aligns actions, visual changes, and
observation-grounded language in a shared space of short-
horizon behavioral intent. An embodiment-conditioned de-
coder maps this intent back to robot-specific actions, allow-
ing a shared representation to support different control in-
terfaces. After pretraining on eight embodiments, CIALLO
adapts to a new robot from one recording of at most 10
seconds by updating only its embodiment embeddings. The
calibrated codec then remains frozen while a policy learns
to predict intent from observations and instructions. We
instantiate this policy with Qwen3.5-4B to form Qwen-
CIALLO, whose backbone requires no large-scale robot-
action pretraining before policy finetuning. With 20 demon-
strations per task, Qwen-CIALLO achieves 76.7% average
success across real SO-101 and single-arm Piper robots,
compared with 70.0% for fully finetuned π0.
In simula-
tion, we additionally finetune the action decoder, achieving
97.3% success on LIBERO and 52.7% on RoboTwin 2.0.
The LIBERO policy also achieves 80.8% on LIBERO-Plus
without further finetuning. Together, these results support a
shared action codec as a transferable control interface that
separates embodiment calibration from task learning.
1. Introduction
A grasp demonstrated by one robot can teach another what
to reach for and how an object should move, even when
their joint coordinates and control rates differ.
Cross-
embodiment learning seeks to reuse experience from exist-
ing robots to provide control knowledge for a new body [14,
27, 32]. Pretrained vision-language models supply broad
semantic knowledge [2, 22], but grounding that knowledge
in an unfamiliar action interface still requires substantial
amounts of robot demonstration data. A reusable action
codec could make these demonstrations more useful for task
learning by reducing the need to adapt the underlying con-
trol representation.
Recorded actions entangle behavior with the coordinates
and control conventions used to execute it. Similar motions
can be expressed as end effector displacements or joint an-
gles, with different dimensions, numbers of arms, and tem-
poral resolutions. Shared action layouts and embodiment-
specific modules allow policies to consume heterogeneous
data [14, 27, 46]. Yet action reconstruction alone, even with
embodiment-conditioned encoding and decoding, does not
require similar behaviors across robots to have similar rep-
resentations. Visual changes and grounded language con-
nect different control sequences that produce similar visual
effects or serve compatible task goals. We therefore align
action representations through these shared cues to learn a
codec that transfers across embodiments with only brief cal-
ibration.
We introduce CIALLO (Cross-embodiment Intent All In
One Space), an action codec for transferring control knowl-
edge across robots. CIALLO separates a shared represen-
tation of short-horizon behavior, intent, from the body and
control schema used to realize it. Building on latent ac-
tion learning and action–vision alignment [5, 6, 49, 52],
CIALLO aligns actions, visual changes, and observation-
grounded language in one space.
The visual and text
branches anchor actions to their effects and task context, en-
couraging nearby representations for compatible behaviors
across embodiments. A shared decoder reconstructs actions
from all three representations, preserving the control infor-
mation needed to execute them (Figure 1A). An essential
vector E, arm and axis embeddings, and execution times
supply the embodiment-specific context. The same codec

2. Related Work
2.1. Vision-Language-Action Models and World
Models
Language-conditioned robot policies map observations and
instructions to actions [3, 14].
Vision-language-action
(VLA) models often build on pretrained vision-language
backbones [2, 22, 31, 37].
World models learn dynam-
ics for behavior learning [18] or controllable video gener-
ation [4, 17, 25]. Video-action models jointly learn pre-
diction and control [55], while JEPA-VLA transfers frozen
video-predictive features into a policy [29].
Fast-WAM
studies the value of predictive representation learning with-
out test-time future imagination [51].
CIALLO focuses
on a shared intent interface with explicit embodiment-
conditioned encoding and decoding.
2.2. Cross-Embodiment Policy Learning
Pooling robot demonstrations [32] requires handling dif-
ferent action dimensions, semantics, and control rates.
Approaches include padded unified action spaces [27],
embodiment- or action-space-specific modules [12, 14, 31],
and embodiment-specific stems feeding a shared trunk [46].
CIALLO conditions shared encoding and decoding net-
works on an essential vector and control schema. Adap-
tation fits the new robot’s essential vector and arm and axis
embeddings, then freezes the calibrated codec during policy
learning.
2.3. Aligning Actions with Visual Change
Inverse dynamics models recover actions from observation
pairs, enabling video labeling from limited action super-
vision [1]. Latent-action methods learn a bottleneck be-
2


tween frames without action labels [4, 40, 49]. UniVLA
learns task-centered latent actions for transfer across em-
bodiments [5]. CLAP uses contrastive action–vision align-
ment [52], while UniT uses cross-reconstruction to unify
visual and kinematic representations [6]. CIALLO aligns
actions, visual change, and observation-grounded language,
with a shared action decoder supervising all three paths un-
der the target embodiment context.
2.4. Action Representations
Diffusion and flow-matching heads model action distribu-
tions through iterative sampling [2, 9, 27]. Efficient alter-
natives include direct regression [21].
Compact policies
such as TinyVLA reduce model cost while retaining dif-
fusion decoding [47]. Discrete representations include per-
dimension bins [3], frequency-domain action tokens [36],
and hierarchical vector quantization [23]. Other approaches
use spatially structured grids or heatmaps [15, 20, 41, 48].
Drawing on SURE’s use of transposed convolutions across
neighboring action bins [53], CIALLO decodes shared
intent into continuous actions through an embodiment-
conditioned head with a softmax expectation readout.
3. Method
3.1. Shared Intent and Embodiment Parameteriza-
tion
A training example contains an instruction, RGB obser-
vations, proprioception, and an action chunk at:t+T −1 ∈
RT ×A together with its execution times. The chunk length,
control rate, and semantics of the A action axes vary across
datasets. CIALLO therefore represents a chunk as a masked
set of timestamped control scalars rather than assigning se-
mantic meaning to the position of a value in one global ac-
tion vector. It compresses this set into an intent z ∈RK×D,
with K = 2 tokens of width D = 512 in our experiments.
The compact bottleneck provides the same interface for ev-
ery embodiment, while the encoders and decoder receive
the body and control context separately. The text branch
also maintains a four-token task-level intent that summa-
rizes the instruction. Combining it with the current obser-
vation produces a chunk-level intent.
CIALLO makes the complementary, body-specific part
of the factorization explicit. Each robot is assigned an es-
sential vector E ∈Rn, with n = 128 in practice. Axis em-
beddings identify whether a scalar controls an end effector
component, a joint coordinate, or a gripper command. Arm
embeddings further specify the manipulator to which the
scalar belongs. Together, these codes provide the informa-
tion needed to translate between a robot’s native signals and
the shared intent space. They also provide the parameters
used for adaptation. For an unseen robot, we estimate a new
E together with its arm and axis embeddings while keeping
the shared network fixed, as illustrated in Figure 1C.
The three encoders produce representations of the same
demonstrated chunk,

3.2. The Action Codec
The action codec implements both qa and pa with pa-
rameters shared across embodiments, as illustrated in Fig-
ure 2. The encoder represents each valid action scalar by
its value, execution time, control axis type, and arm iden-
tity. Embodiment-conditioned blocks map these tokens into
the common feature space, after which the Query Pooling
module compresses the entire chunk into K intent tokens.
A separate proprioceptive encoder summarizes the current
body state and injects it through a learned gate. The re-
sulting intent is grounded primarily in the executed motion,
while proprioception supplies the state and coordinate con-
text needed to interpret that motion.
The decoder reverses this mapping for a requested target
interface. The Time Query module reads the intent at the
target execution times. It combines the resulting features

3.3. Vision Encoder
The vision path recovers intent over a short horizon from
the change between the current and future observations, as
4


illustrated in Figure 3.
Both frames from every camera
are processed by the same frozen DINOv2 backbone [33].
Freezing this backbone preserves its semantic representa-
tion while the interaction encoder learns from heteroge-
neous robot data. These backbone features remain fixed
during embodiment calibration, while the downstream vi-
sual representation can change through its dependence on
E. The paired patch features are concatenated along the
channel dimension and processed by a two-stage Con-
vNeXt interaction encoder conditioned on the essential vec-
tor E [28]. This local hierarchy is well suited to the small
visual changes produced over a short action chunk. It avoids
patch-level global attention and a full cost volume while re-
taining a sufficient receptive field to relate nearby changes.
The resulting tokens from each available view receive a
camera embedding. A validity mask removes absent cam-
eras and padded tokens, so the same encoder handles vari-
able camera counts. Query Pooling summarizes all valid
views into the K ×D intent interface shared with the action
path. Its learned queries select task-relevant changes across
cameras without forming all patch pairs. The DINOv2 fea-
tures for the current frame are also retained for the text path.
Input resolution, stage dimensions, mask definitions, and
complexity are detailed in Supplementary Section A.3.
3.4. Text Codec
An instruction usually describes an entire task, whereas
chunk-level intent specifies what should happen over the
next short horizon. The text path separates task understand-
ing from temporal grounding to bridge these two scopes, as
illustrated in Figure 3. A frozen BERT encoder [11] rep-
resents the instruction, and Query Pooling compresses its
valid token features into a task-level intent. These task-
level tokens are fused with visual tokens extracted from
the current frame by the frozen vision backbone. A sec-
ond Query Pooling module extracts K grounded tokens,
and two Transformer Layers refine them into a chunk-level
intent in RK×D. This design uses the current observation
to identify instruction content relevant to the present chunk,
with little additional visual computation.
The task-level intent bottleneck is trained through
masked text reconstruction. The Masked Decoder receives
an instruction with selected tokens hidden and attends to the
task-level intent as its latent memory. The loss is evaluated
only at the masked positions. Reconstructing the missing
content encourages the task-level intent bottleneck to pre-
serve distinctions expressed by the instruction.
The text
path intentionally differs from the action and vision paths
in its conditioning inputs. It is neither conditioned on E nor
given proprioception because language specifies the task in-
dependently of the body that performs it. The current ob-
servation supplies visual grounding, while alignment across
modalities connects the resulting intent to an embodiment-
relevant realization.
Architectural details and the recon-
struction objective are given in Supplementary Section A.4.
3.5. Supervision
The supervision structure is summarized in Figure 1A. The
action, vision, and text paths each pass their intent through
the same action decoder and reconstruct the demonstrated
action chunk with masked ℓ1 loss.
Sharing the decoder
makes alignment functional rather than purely geometric.
A visual or textual intent must expose the same informa-
tion that the action codec needs to recover the embodiment-
specific controls under the corresponding target context.
The task-level intent is additionally supervised by
masked text reconstruction, as described in Section 3.4. Fi-
nally, symmetric InfoNCE losses [39] align the three pairs
of representations using negatives from the global batch.

3.6. Policy Learning and Embodiment Calibration
With the action codec pretrained, we can train a policy to
predict intent from observations and instructions, as illus-
trated in Figure 1B. Qwen-CIALLO uses a Qwen3.5-4B
backbone followed by an intent projector to produce this
K × D representation. The frozen action encoder provides
teacher tokens from the demonstrated actions, which super-
vise the predicted intent through a cosine alignment loss.
We also decode the predicted tokens and apply an action
regression loss against the demonstrations, training the pol-
icy to produce intent that supports accurate control. For the
simulation benchmarks, we jointly finetune the backbone,
projector, and action decoder. At inference, the backbone
predicts intent directly, so the teacher encoder is no longer
needed.
When transferring to a new robot, we first fit the em-
bodiment parameters that allow it to use the pretrained
codec. As shown in Figure 1C, we calibrate its essential
vector and arm and axis embeddings from a recording of at
most 10 seconds while keeping the shared network weights
5


fixed. This optimization takes approximately 30 minutes
on one GPU. We then freeze the calibrated codec and train
the backbone and projector on demonstrations of the new
robot’s tasks. Keeping the codec fixed during this second
stage lets us evaluate whether it can support policy learning
after calibration alone. The real-robot evaluation reports the
calibration recording and the policy demonstrations sepa-
rately to distinguish the data used for these two stages.
4. Experiments
We first evaluate whether the three intent paths align and
retain information needed by the shared action decoder.
We then evaluate policies trained through this interface on
LIBERO and RoboTwin 2.0, with LIBERO-Plus testing the
LIBERO policy under distribution shifts. Finally, we assess
adaptation to real robots by calibrating the codec and keep-
ing it frozen during policy training.
4.1. CIALLO Pretraining
We pretrain CIALLO on 125,434 episodes from eight em-
bodiments, with balanced sampling across embodiments.
The data span LIBERO [26], Bridge and Fractal as used
by SimplerEnv [24], and RoboTwin 2.0 [7].
On 2,048 held-out samples, action intent retrieves its
paired visual or grounded language intent, and vice versa,
with bidirectional top-1 accuracies of 96.68% and 91.94%,
respectively. Retrieval between vision and grounded lan-
guage reaches 96.88%.
The shared decoder also recon-
structs actions from each modality, with normalized masked
MAE of 0.00761 from action, 0.01853 from vision, and
0.02429 from grounded language.
Supplementary Sec-
tion B details training, evaluation, and results by embodi-
ment.
4.2. Contrastive Alignment Ablation
We vary the contrastive terms while retaining all reconstruc-
tion losses. Each model uses eight embodiments, two intent
tokens, and the same 2,000-step training budget and valida-
tion set. Action–vision alignment alone yields the lowest
vision-to-action error (Table 1). The full objective yields
the lowest action-to-action error, a 7.1% reduction relative
to reconstruction alone, and also improves vision-to-action
reconstruction over that baseline.

4.4. Number of Pretraining Embodiments
With the training objective and step budget fixed, both
action-to-action and vision-to-action MAE decrease on
each of the three original embodiments (Panda, WidowX,
and Google Robot) as pretraining expands from three to five
to eight embodiments. For Panda, action-to-action MAE
falls from 0.0807 to 0.0339 to 0.0136. Each expansion also
introduces more diverse demonstrations from RoboTwin.
We hypothesize that training on more embodiments and a
wider range of demonstrations helps the shared codec rep-
resent a broader range of actions, which could explain the
lower reconstruction errors even on the original three em-
bodiments. Supplementary Table 8 gives per-embodiment
results and five-embodiment validation counts.
4.5. Direct Closed-Loop Control
For closed-loop control without policy finetuning, we feed

4.7. Adaptation to New Embodiments
Evaluation protocol. We evaluate adaptation on two real
robot platforms, SO-101 and a single-arm Piper, using three
manipulation tasks with 20 demonstrations per task on each
robot. Supplementary Section E provides the task instruc-
tions and example observations.
Codec calibration.
From one recording of at most 10
seconds per robot, we fit only its essential vector E and
axis and arm embeddings, keeping all shared codec weights
frozen (Figure 1C). The frozen DINOv2 features are inde-
pendent of E, and visual reconstruction is less sensitive to
encoder mismatch than action reconstruction (Supplemen-
tary Table 10). Aligning the new robot’s action intent to
this visual reference is designed to largely preserve the pre-
trained intent space.
Task learning. We have not pretrained a generalist policy to
predict CIALLO intents. With the calibrated codec frozen,
we therefore train only the Qwen3.5-4B backbone and in-
tent projector on the 20 demonstrations per task to predict
the next intent from the current observation and instruction.
We compare against full finetuning of pretrained π0 [2] on
the same demonstrations and evaluate each method over 20
trials per task on each robot (Table 5). Supplementary Sec-
tion C.2 gives the optimization settings.
Results. With 20 demonstrations per task, Qwen-CIALLO
achieves 76.7% average success, compared with 70.0% for
π0 (Table 5).
The methods tie on beer pouring on both
robots. On water-bottle placement, π0 leads by 5 percentage
points on SO-101 and 10 on Piper. On the wooden-fish task,
Qwen-CIALLO achieves higher success, reaching 70% ver-
sus 35% on SO-101 and 40% versus 20% on Piper. This
task requires grasping a thin screwdriver before striking the
wooden fish, demanding greater grasp precision.
These
results compare two routes to cross-embodiment transfer.
π0 transfers a policy pretrained on large-scale, multi-robot
action data and then fully finetuned on the target tasks.
Qwen-CIALLO transfers the intent space of a separately
pretrained codec and uses the target demonstrations to train
only Qwen3.5-4B and its intent projector to select intents
for the frozen, calibrated codec.

5. Conclusion
We presented CIALLO, an action codec that separates
shared behavioral intent from embodiment-specific control.
During pretraining, visual changes and grounded language
connect actions through shared effects and task context. The
aligned action codec then supports policy learning across
embodiments. On each of two real robots, one short record-
ing calibrates the codec, which remains frozen during pol-
icy learning from 20 demonstrations per task. Together with
the simulation results, these findings support separating em-
bodiment calibration from task learning. Future work will
align human egocentric videos with humanoid actions in the
shared intent space and scale CIALLO pretraining to larger,
more diverse datasets. We also aim to pretrain generalist
vision-language-action policies from vision-language mod-
els using the codec.

A.2.4. Connection to Integral Coordinate Regression
Our readout follows an established approach to coordi-
nate regression. Integral pose regression [45] shows that
a softmax expectation over a heatmap can be trained with
coordinate ℓ1 loss alone, without direct heatmap supervi-
sion.
Expectation-based coordinate readout also appears
in LoFTR’s fine matching stage [44].
DSNT [30] sim-
ilarly provides a parameter-free expectation operator that
connects convolutional heatmaps to continuous coordinates.
These results motivate learning the bin representation indi-
rectly through the decoded action.
The convolutional parameterization supplies a separate
design motivation. Nibali et al. [30] argue that spatial pa-
rameter sharing helps coordinate models generalize across
image locations and that a learned fully connected readout
can compromise this property. In CIALLO, the correspond-
ing axis indexes numerical action values, and the TransConv
Head reuses local kernels along that axis as it expands the
bin resolution. This introduces locality and weight sharing
into the output representation. The analogy motivates our
architecture, but does not establish the image-translation
generalization studied by DSNT for robot actions.
Coordinate supervision does not uniquely determine the
intermediate representation.
DSNT discusses this am-
biguity and explores additional regularization to control
heatmap shape [30]. Bias-Compensated Integral Regres-
sion further identifies decoding bias from the softmax–
expectation combination and slower convergence under im-
plicit heatmap supervision in pose estimation [16]. These
findings caution against interpreting a differentiable readout
as a guarantee of faithful distribution learning or efficient
optimization. CIALLO uses neither heatmap regularization
nor bias compensation. We treat its bin weights as a learned
decoding basis and evaluate the resulting actions, without
assuming a particular distribution shape, number of modes,
or calibrated uncertainty.
Figure 4 illustrates how the bin weights evolve under
scalar supervision in a simplified setting. We pass a fixed in-
put through two transposed convolution layers and a shared
linear projection to produce logits over 16 ordered bins. We
train the network by applying an ℓ1 loss to the softmax ex-
pectation, using noisy labels around a fixed nominal target
of 0.75.

A.4. Text Codec
The frozen BERT encoder produces one feature per instruc-
tion token. A learned linear projection maps these features
to width D = 512, while the instruction attention mask re-
moves padding. Query Pooling uses four learned queries to
compress the valid features into a four-token task-level in-
tent. The projection and pooling modules are trained while
BERT remains frozen.
To ground each chunk, the text path reuses the DINOv2
features F t
c from the current frame in Eq. 14, before the
interaction encoder for the frame pair. Each camera con-
tributes 324 patch tokens from the current frame. These
tokens are projected to width D, augmented with camera
and visual modality embeddings, and concatenated with the
task-level intent after adding a task modality embedding.
The combined validity mask keeps all four task-level in-
tent tokens and only the patches from available cameras.
A second Query Pooling module reduces this sequence to
K = 2 tokens, and two Transformer Layers produce the
chunk-level intent in RK×D. This path receives neither the
essential vector E nor proprioception.
The two-layer Masked Decoder receives the corrupted
instruction with learned position embeddings and attends
to the task-level intent as memory. Its vocabulary projec-
tion is tied to the frozen BERT input embedding, and cross-
entropy is averaged only over the selected masked positions.
The chunk-level intent is not used as decoder memory. This
direct reconstruction path forces the task-level intent to pre-
serve instruction content rather than only the identity of a
task class.

B. Pretraining Diagnostics
Data and training setup. We pretrain CIALLO on 125,434
episodes comprising 1,354 Panda episodes from the four
LIBERO suites [26], 45,541 WidowX episodes from Bridge
and 69,339 Google Robot episodes from Fractal as used by
SimplerEnv [24], and 9,200 episodes from five RoboTwin
2.0 embodiments [7]. The RoboTwin portion covers Aloha
AgileX, Franka Dual, ARX X5, Piper, and UR5. Every
source uses only its main camera. We balance sampling
across the eight embodiments. The maximum and mini-
mum sample counts differ by 0.30% of the mean count.
Training runs for 16,000 optimizer steps with a global
batch size of 1,024, which processes 16.38 million exam-
ples. We use AdamW with weight decay 0.01, a peak learn-
ing rate of 3 × 10−4, 400 warmup steps, cosine decay, and
BF16 precision. The model has 262.05M parameters, of
which 65.99M are trainable because DINOv2 and BERT re-
main frozen. Training takes 15.7 hours on 8 NVIDIA H20
GPUs, including validation and checkpoint writes.
B.1. Pretraining Results
We evaluate the final checkpoint from one pretraining seed
using an episode-level validation split. The evaluation in-
cludes 2,048 held-out samples from 1,575 episodes and 512
tasks. Each 2×512 intent is flattened in slot order, and exact
nearest neighbors are found with raw ℓ2 distance. Cross-
modal retrieval measures how accurately each representa-
tion retrieves its paired demonstration sample. We denote
reconstruction from action, visual, and grounded language
intent by AA, VA, and TA, respectively. The language rep-
resentation is the chunk-level intent conditioned on the cur-
rent observation, distinct from the four-token task-level in-
tent used for instruction reconstruction.
Table 7 reports retrieval within each embodiment and
reconstruction through the shared decoder.
The higher
VA and TA errors for Panda, WidowX, and Google Robot
may reflect differences in task coverage, camera setup,
control rate, action normalization, and environment. Per-
embodiment MAE should therefore not be interpreted as
a ranking of representation quality. In the All row, MAE
is aggregated over valid action elements, while retrieval
searches the complete sample pool.
B.2. Number of Pretraining Embodiments
We compare pretraining on three, five, and eight embodi-

B.6. Embodiment Conditioning
We test the functional role of the essential vector on the
same 2,048 validation samples used in Section 4.1. Each
sample is assigned the next embodiment in a fixed cycle
from Panda through WidowX, Google Robot, Aloha Ag-
ileX, Franka Dual, ARX X5, Piper, and UR5 before return-
ing to Panda. We keep the action, observations, instruc-
tion, proprioception, timestamps, masks, and target action
schema unchanged. The wrong encoder condition replaces
E only in the action or visual encoder and its propriocep-
tive context. The wrong decoder condition replaces E only
in the shared action decoder. The all wrong condition ap-
plies both replacements. This protocol tests conditioning
under a controlled mismatch. It does not retarget an action
to another robot.
Table 10 shows that the wrong decoder condition raises
MAE for all three paths to approximately 0.19–0.21. The
wrong action encoder also raises AA MAE from 0.00761
to 0.04506. Replacing the visual encoder condition has a
smaller effect on VA, whose MAE rises from 0.01853 to
0.02189. The text path does not consume E or propriocep-
tion, so its wrong encoder value is unchanged by construc-
tion. These results show that the action encoder and decoder
use the essential vector, while the visual path remains sen-
sitive to it to a lesser degree. The errors need not increase
monotonically across conditions because simultaneous en-
coder and decoder mismatches can partially compensate for
one another.
C. Training Details
C.1. Qwen-CIALLO
For LIBERO, we train a single Qwen-CIALLO policy
jointly on all four task suites for 10,000 optimization steps
on 8 NVIDIA H20 GPUs. The per-device batch size is 16.
We use AdamW with weight decay 10−8, 5,000 warmup
steps, and cosine annealing. The Qwen3.5-4B backbone,
CIALLO action decoder, and intent projector use learning
rates of 2 × 10−5, 2 × 10−6, and 1 × 10−4, respectively.
For RoboTwin 2.0, we train a single Qwen-CIALLO pol-
icy jointly across all 50 tasks for 40,000 optimization steps
on 8 NVIDIA H20 GPUs. The per-device batch size is 4.
We otherwise use the LIBERO training configuration, in-
cluding AdamW with weight decay 10−8, 5,000 warmup
steps, cosine annealing, and learning rates of 2 × 10−5 for
the Qwen3.5-4B backbone, 2×10−6 for the CIALLO action
decoder, and 1 × 10−4 for the intent projector.

D. Action Head Efficiency
We compare the direct point-regression head used by
OpenVLA-OFT [21], the flow-matching head of π0.5 [37],
and the CIALLO action decoder.
CIALLO uses a
distribution-based regression readout that predicts softmax
weights over 256 ordered bins and outputs their expecta-
tion in a single forward pass (Eq. 12). The bin weights are
learned through action regression and serve as a structured
decoding basis, rather than a calibrated action distribution.
Benchmark protocol. All heads are benchmarked on a sin-
gle NVIDIA H20 GPU in BF16 precision, with batch size 1,
an action horizon of 8 steps, and action dimension 7. We re-
port mean latency over 100 inference runs after 20 warmup
runs. Table 11 reports head parameter counts, FLOPs, and
inference latency, excluding the policy backbone. For com-
parison within the framework used by π0.5, we also imple-
ment the CIALLO decoder in JAX with the same architec-
ture and action readout. This version is labeled “(JAX)”.
