HumiMimic: Mimetic Embodiment Transfer of Human–Humanoid
Interaction from In-the-Wild Videos

Fig. 1: Overview of HumiTransfer: Given an in-the-wild human–human interaction video, our mimetic embodiment transfer replaces one
participant with a humanoid while preserving the interaction semantics and dynamics that characterize the original human behavior. Through
this, HumiTransfer ensures that the resulting behavior is physically plausible and compatible with different humanoid embodiments.
Abstract— Enabling a humanoid to interact naturally with
human partners requires preserving not only individual mo-
tions but also the spatial, semantic, and contact relationships
underlying the interaction (i.e., hugging, dancing, hand-shaking,
etc.). Furthermore, a practical solution should generalize across
various humanoid embodiments with distinct morphologies
and kinematic constraints. To tackle this challenge, we pro-
pose HumiTransfer, a mimetic embodiment transfer frame-
work that converts in-the-wild human-human interaction videos
into physically plausible human-humanoid interactions. Hu-
miTransfer first reconstructs 3D human motion with con-
sistent foot-ground contact, then transfers one participant
to a target humanoid embodiment via interaction modeling,
using weighted joint-relative directions and anatomical offsets
to prioritize interaction-critical inter-body relationships, while
post-retargeting optimization enforces balance, contact, and
collision avoidance. Subsequently, we apply HumiTransfer to
1, 200 in-the-wild videos, producing approximately 500, 000
frames of diverse interactions for the 26-DoF Unitree R1 Edu
and the 23-DoF HighTorque Mini-hi humanoid robot, resulting
in our HumiMimic dataset. Across both robots, HumiTrans-
fer achieves superior motion fidelity, preserves inter-body rela-
tionships, and maintains contact accuracy compared with exist-
ing baselines. Moreover, we demonstrate that HumiMimic sup-
ports motion generation methods for synthesizing interactive
humanoid motions following a human partner. Finally, our
real robot deployments confirm the physical executability of
the transferred motions in real-world settings. Our project is
available at https://aioznetwork.github.io/HumiMimic/.

I. INTRODUCTION
Humans are adept at participating in the social world not
only through explicit instruction but also by observing and
responding to others’ behaviors [1]. Such a capacity is com-
monly referred to as mimesis: the representation and commu-
nication of experience through interaction in both semantic
and dynamic manners [2], [3]. Indeed, interaction cannot be
acquired by imitating isolated motions alone. Meaningful
human-to-human interactions fundamentally arise from the
evolving relationship between participants. For example,
making a humanoid dance with another human requires not
merely reproducing the learned whole-body motions, but also
being aware of interpersonal spacing and orientation, syn-
chronizing their rhythm and movement phase, coordinating
contact, and continually adapting to the partner’s motion
despite variations in embodiment. Herein, mimesis offers
a natural account of how observed human behavior can
translate into actions for humanoid robots, connecting motion
recognition, motion generation, and embodied communica-
tion, thus opening up a vast range of applications in human-
humanoid interactions.
In practice, existing humanoid motion datasets [10], [13],
[14] are predominantly collected through high-fidelity tele-
operation or motion-capture systems in controlled environ-
ments. Although these pipelines provide accurate, robot-
compatible motions, they are often tied to specific embod-
iments and capture only a subset of interaction behaviors
within limited environments. Advances in scene-aware and
occlusion-robust human pose estimation [15], [16], mesh-
based body fitting [17], and learning-based monocular re-
construction [18] have made it possible to recover whole-
body human motion from videos. Despite promising results,
they often overlook human-human interactions due to the
scarcity of suitable datasets. Publicly available videos, by

Recent advances in whole-body motion learning [19]–
[21] have expanded the range of human-like behaviors that
humanoids can synthesize. Still, learning behaviors such
as human-to-human interactions remain fundamentally con-
strained by the availability and diversity of training data:
a humanoid must not only reproduce an individual motion
but also coordinate with its human partner [22]. Meanwhile,
retargeting methods offer a means of converting observed
human behavior into embodiment-specific compatibility, yet
existing approaches [23]–[25] primarily optimize single-
agent motion, neither preserving the inter-body relationships
underlying the interaction nor identifying which semantic
cues to optimize across embodiments. Therefore, the central
challenge of mimetic embodiment transfer is to translate
one participant’s motion into a humanoid embodiment while
preserving the interaction semantics with its human partner.
To tackle this challenge, we propose HumiTransfer, a new
mimetic embodiment transfer framework that translates in-
the-wild human-human interactions into human-humanoid
interactions, as shown in Fig. 1. HumiTransfer takes various
interaction sequences as video frames to extract human
motion, then transfers one human participant’s poses to
two distinct humanoid embodiments: the 23-DoF High-
Torque Mini-hi and the 26-DoF Unitree R1 Edu, resulting
in our HumiMimic dataset. We also benchmark existing
retargeting methods to showcase the diversity and complex-
ity of interactions in HumiMimic. In summary, our main
contributions are twofold as follows:
• We propose HumiTransfer, a mimetic embodiment
transfer framework that translates one human into a
humanoid while preserving the semantics and distances
underlying the interaction and satisfying embodiment-
specific kinematic and physical constraints.
• We introduce HumiMimic, a scalable embodied dataset
that contains 1, 200 human-humanoid interaction se-
quences (∼500, 000 frames) by extracting body frames
from in-the-wild human-human interaction videos. Our
code and dataset will be made publicly available.
II. RELATED WORK
Human Motion Datasets. Human motion datasets have
traditionally relied on motion capture systems in controlled
environments [26], [27]. Human3.6M [5] and CMU Mo-
Cap [28] provide accurate 3D poses, while AMASS [4]
unifies diverse MoCap sources in the SMPL represen-
tation [29].
Multi-person interaction
datasets, such
as
CHI3D [30] and Harmony4D [8], introduce physical in-
teractions, while 3DPW [7] and KIT Whole-Body [6] in-
corporate more unconstrained settings. Recent humanoid
datasets, H2O [10], AgiBot World [11], CLONED [12],
and OmniH2O-6 [13], provide humanoid-compatible mo-
tion but are primarily collected through teleoperation or
controlled data acquisition. In general, existing datasets
lack the joint provision of large-scale, in-the-wild multi-
person interactions and humanoid-compatible motion. To fill
this gap, this work introduces HumiMimic, a dataset of
human-humanoid interactions featuring high-fidelity, phys-
ically plausible poses/motions.
Human-to-Humanoid
Retargeting. Human-to-humanoid
retargeting ranges from inverse kinematics and optimization-
based methods [23], [31] to learning-based approaches [32],
[33]. Optimization-based approaches primarily preserve the
motion of the transferred agent through joint or end-effector
alignment [10], [34], [35]. Uniform transfer [25] preserves
relative geometry through scaling, yet it is sensitive to
embodiment differences. Proportional transfer [10], [23]
adapts motion to the target embodiment but introduces
distortions due to morphological dissimilarities. Under the
umbrella of interactive robotics, these methods fall short
of determining the relational cues critical to interaction,
making it difficult to maintain high-fidelity, embodiment-
specific interactions. Meanwhile, physics-based approaches
improve the geometric quality of interactions [36], where
recent interaction-aware approaches [24], [37] explicitly
consider relationships between interacting bodies, while
graphics-oriented methods [38], [39] preserve interaction
semantics. Within this research scope, HumiTransfer extends
optimization-based human-to-humanoid retargeting, enabling
embodiment-specific interaction with another human partic-
ipant in in-the-wild settings.

III. HUMITRANSFER: MIMETIC EMBODIMENT
TRANSFER FOR HUMAN-HUMANOID INTERACTION
As shown in Fig. 2, we introduce a three-stage framework:
(i) a framework for human–humanoid interaction with recon-
structed 3D interaction motions; (ii) HumiTransfer, a mimetic
embodiment transfer for interaction-preserving human-to-
humanoid motion retargeting; and (iii) a post-retargeting
optimization stage. After which, we obtain HumiMimic, our
human-humanoid interaction dataset.
A. Human-Humanoid Interaction Data
1) Data Collection & Statistics: We gather high-quality
videos
of
in-the-wild
human-human
interactions
from
YouTube, TikTok, Facebook, and other Internet sources,
involving only two participant subjects. As depicted in Fig. 3,
our resulting dataset contains 1, 200 two-person interaction
videos, yielding about 500, 000 processed frames (approxi-
mately 4.6 video hours). Each sequence ranges from 100 to
1, 000 frames, with more than half exceeding 300 frames.
Regarding diversity, our dataset covers a broad spectrum of
interactions, such as co-movement (29%), social exchanges
(21%), dual sports (17%), and sparring (13%).
2) Extraction of Human-Human Interaction Motion: As
we noticed image-quality distortions, camera instability, and
scene complexity in the collected in-the-wild videos, we per-
form an automatic filtering step [40] to remove low-fidelity
frames and re-encapsulate high-fidelity ones, retaining ap-
proximately 73% of all frames. Next, in each re-encapsulated
interaction sequence, we employ a multi-person tracker [41]
to detect humans in each frame using bounding boxes. For
each bounding box, a pre-trained keypoint detector [42] is
used to detect the human’s key positions within the bounding
box, which are subsequently used to perform SMPL recon-
struction [43]. Thus, to ensure the physical plausibility of
extracted poses, we optimize two complementary objectives
in each video: (a) whole-body shape-fitting optimization and
(b) foot-ground contact consistency fine-tuning.
a) Whole-Body Shape Fitting: The pose parameter (α)
and the body shape parameter (β) of the SMPL model [43]

C. Post-Retargeting Interaction Optimization
Yet, these newly retargeted poses face challenges in self-
balancing and inter-body collisions. We mitigate these arti-
facts by optimizing post-retargeting interactions as we roll
out their poses. For self-balancing, we follow the full-body
compliant human-humanoid interaction mechanism [49]. To
prevent inter-body collisions, we maintain an inter-body
minimum safe distance of dmin,ij between JR
i,t and JP
j,t at
each given discrete timestep t. We apply this post-retargeting
optimization throughout the human-humanoid interaction
horizon for all human-humanoid interaction sequences.
Lastly, we apply the entire HumiTransfer framework to
all human-human interaction sequences, yielding the fi-
nal HumiMimic, which is used in our experiments.
IV. EXPERIMENT
We conduct intensive experiments to: (i) evaluate the
effectiveness of HumiTransfer; (ii) demonstrate the utility of
our HumiMimic dataset for a generative human-humanoid
interaction task; and (iii) validate real-world deployment
by using a trained policy on our HumiMimic dataset and
executing it on a real humanoid robot.
A. Assessment of Extraction & Retargeting in HumiTransfer
First, we assess the performance of our HumiTransfer in
the human-to-humanoid motion transfer task. As no training
is involved, all experiments in this section run on our
full HumiMimic dataset.

teraction:

extracted human-human interactions (Eq. 3) by computing
their foot-slipping errors (Efoot) [50], ground penetration
(Eground) [51], and static stability margin (SSM) [52]. As
in [53], we compute SSM as the signed distance between the
projected center of mass (CoM) and the support polygon.

2) Real-Robot Deployment: To evaluate real-world exe-
cutability, we deploy the retargeted motions on the 26-DoF
Unitree R1 Edu. We use the sequences from HumiMimic to
optimize and map to the robot’s joints, then check against
the robot’s motion constraints before execution. In particular,
we employ the Unitree R1 Edu motion-tracking controller
in mjlab [61], thus optimizing a 26-action policy in 4096
parallel environments for approximately 30, 000 iterations on
an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU.
The training stage samples initial motion phases, perturbs
the robot’s initial pose and velocity, and randomizes phys-
ical parameters, including foot friction and joint dynamics.
Following DeepMimic [33] and BeyondMimic [62], we con-
dition the actor on this command and proprioception, reward-
ing the simulated robot for matching the reference. At each
simulated step, a motion command samples joint positions
and velocities to form the policy command, while anchor
and whole-body states supply the corresponding tracking
targets. The trained actor is then exported to ONNX and
executed in the C++ mimic controller with ONNX Runtime.
At 50 Hz, the controller reads the 26 motor states and IMU
from Unitree low-state messages, reconstructs the training
observation order, and finally evaluates the policy. For de-
tails, the observations contain reference joint positions and
velocities, torso orientation error, IMU angular velocity, and
the previous motion. The torso orientation is reconstructed
from the IMU quaternion and waist joints without global
position estimation. A fixed mapping converts the common
26-joint policy order to the sparse Unitree SDK motor IDs
used on the robot platform. Fig. 8 shows that our framework
preserves the characteristic spatial and contact relationships
in retargeted human-human interactions. The demonstration
video is available in our supplementary materials.
D. Limitations & Future Work
Nevertheless, HumiTransfer remains limited by substantial
morphological differences between humans and humanoids,
which can make certain motions kinematically infeasible. For
example, in simulation, we found that the HighTorque Mini-
hi’s broad shoulders and short arms prevent it from hugging


a human partner as the Unitree R1 Edu can. Furthermore, our
current real-robot deployment primarily relies on predefined
motion sequences and does not yet explicitly account for
human motion. An interesting direction for future work is
therefore to develop methods that adapt humanoid motion
in response to observed human behavior, leveraging motion
data from our HumiMimic dataset.
V. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we have presented HumiTransfer for mimetic
embodiment transfer from in-the-wild human-human inter-
action videos. Our proposed method includes a data prepa-
ration pipeline that extracts physically plausible human-
human interaction poses from in-the-wild videos, followed
by a mimetic embodiment transfer that translates one of the
observed humans into a humanoid. Our HumiMimic dataset
demonstrates higher quality in avoiding ground penetra-
tion and foot slipping, while HumiTransfer allows human
poses to be retargeted to different humanoids. Benchmarking
on HumiMimic across motion generation methods and
deployments on the Unitree R1 Edu humanoid showed that
our work enables physical deployment and can serve as a
benchmark for future human-humanoid interaction work.
