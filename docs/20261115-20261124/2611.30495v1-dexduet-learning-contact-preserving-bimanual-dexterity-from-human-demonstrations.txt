Abstract
Human demonstrations provide a rich source of bimanual
dexterous skills, yet transferring them to robot hands re-
mains challenging.
Existing methods primarily optimize
object trajectory tracking, often overlooking how human
hands interact with the object. Even with accurate tracking,
robots may contact incorrect object parts or slide their fin-
gers, resulting in unstable manipulation. We present Dex-
Duet, a framework that learns contact-preserving biman-
ual dexterity from a single human demonstration.
Dex-
Duet employs two interaction rewards to preserve human
hand-object configurations and contacts.
To enable co-
ordinated manipulation, we factorize the bimanual pol-
icy into two weight-sharing per-hand policies that condi-
tion on each other’s states. We further propose assisted
reference-state initialization (A-RSI), an adaptive curricu-
lum that adjusts object assistance and training starts across
demonstration segments to overcome difficult manipulation
phases.
Across four benchmarks spanning three simula-
tors and five robot embodiments, DexDuet improves object-
tracking ADD-AUC by 11–41 points over the correspond-
ing baselines while consistently achieving better contact
preservation. Notably, among 122 paired runs with compa-
rable object-tracking accuracy, DexDuet preserves the hu-
man’s contacts better in 117 cases. Code and videos are
available on our project page.
1. Introduction
People routinely manipulate objects with both hands, such
as holding a bottle while opening its lid. Learning such
dexterous skills from human demonstrations offers a nat-
ural approach to robotic manipulation [5, 36, 37, 46]. How-
ever, differences between human and robot hands prevent
demonstrations from being directly replayed on robots. Re-
cent methods bridge this gap by retargeting human motions
to robot hands and training policies in simulation to track

demonstrated object trajectories [21, 30, 63]. Nevertheless,
a fundamental limitation of these methods is their reliance
on object trajectory tracking as the primary learning ob-
jective. Even when a robot accurately tracks the demon-
strated trajectory, its hands may contact the wrong object
parts or slide on the object, resulting in unstable manipula-
tion. Faithful bimanual manipulation requires not only re-
producing where the object moves, but also preserving how
the hands interact with it.
However, achieving contact-preserving bimanual ma-
nipulation presents three key challenges.
First, biman-
ual manipulation requires coordinated yet role-specific con-
trol. Existing methods either control both hands through a
unified policy [30, 63] or train separate policies indepen-
dently [62], limiting explicit coordination or shared learn-
ing between hands. Second, preserving human hand-object
contacts across different embodiments remains difficult.
Although existing methods incorporate contact-related re-
wards [22, 30, 63], robot hands can still contact the object
differently from human hands, particularly in which hand
holds an object part and whether the contact is maintained.
Third, learning complex demonstrations is hindered by dif-
ficult manipulation phases. To make training tractable, prior
methods assist the object early in training, with a virtual ob-
ject controller (VOC), a proportional-derivative (PD) con-
troller that pulls the object toward its demonstrated trajec-
tory [30, 63], or with relaxed physics [21], and gradually
withdraw this assistance. However, the assistance is typi-
cally reduced uniformly across the demonstration, allowing
a difficult phase, such as a handover, to stall the entire train-
ing process.
To address these challenges, we introduce DexDuet, a
framework for learning contact-preserving bimanual dex-
terity from a single human demonstration (Fig. 1). To co-
ordinate the two hands, DexDuet factorizes the bimanual
policy into two per-hand policies that share network param-
eters while observing each other’s states. This allows each
hand to perform its own role, adapt to its partner, and bene-
fit from shared learning. To preserve human hand-object
interactions, DexDuet introduces two complementary in-
teraction rewards.
The first encourages each robot hand
to match the orientation and finger configuration of its hu-
man counterpart relative to the object. The second encour-
ages each hand region, such as the palm or a finger, to con-
tact the object where the corresponding human region does.
Together, these rewards guide the robot to reproduce the
demonstrated contacts rather than merely track object mo-
tion. Furthermore, to facilitate learning through difficult
manipulation phases, we propose assisted reference-state
initialization (A-RSI), an adaptive curriculum that adjusts
VOC assistance separately for each demonstration segment
based on its learning progress. It also initializes episodes
from previously reached states and prioritizes failing seg-
ments, enabling the policy to learn complex demonstrations
without assistance. In summary, our contributions are:
• We present DexDuet, a framework for learning contact-
preserving bimanual dexterity from human demonstra-
tions through factorized, weight-sharing per-hand poli-
cies that enable role-specific control and partner-aware
coordination.
• We introduce two complementary interaction rewards
that preserve human hand-object interactions by aligning
object-relative hand configurations and region-level con-
tacts across different robot embodiments.
• We propose assisted reference-state initialization (A-
RSI), an adaptive curriculum that combines segment-
specific VOC assistance, initialization from previously
reached states, and failure-aware sampling to improve
learning through difficult manipulation phases.
• We evaluate DexDuet on four benchmarks spanning three
simulators and five robot embodiments, demonstrating
consistent improvements in object tracking and contact
preservation, with additional demonstrations on two real
robot systems.
2. Related Work
Learning dexterous manipulation from human demon-
strations.
Human demonstrations are a natural source of
dexterous skills, because people handle everyday objects
with both hands skillfully. Many works therefore collect
demonstrations of hand-object interaction.
They capture
them in motion-capture and multi-camera studios [2, 6, 43]
or with wearable gloves, exoskeletons and headsets [1,
44, 46, 51], reconstruct them from monocular or egocen-
tric videos [7, 33, 36, 59], or generate them with models
trained on such data [58]. These efforts have produced large
datasets of hand-object interaction [9, 16, 26, 60]. How-
ever, these demonstrations cannot be replayed directly on
a robot, because robot hands differ from human hands in
size, kinematics and actuation. For humanoid robots and
physics-based characters, a common solution to this gap
maps the human motion onto the target body and trains a
policy in physics simulation to track it [14, 23, 47, 52]. Re-
cent methods apply the same idea to dexterous hands. They
train policies that move the object along its demonstrated
trajectory, guided by the retargeted hand motion [5, 8, 21,
22, 25, 27, 30, 38, 53, 63], or optimize the control sequence
directly [24, 34]. These methods are primarily driven by
an object-tracking reward. Some of them also compare the
robot’s contacts with the human’s frame by frame, either
by their locations [19, 22, 30] or by the forces and torques
they can exert on the object [63]. These terms act on indi-
vidual contacts in each frame, and none of them considers
whether a contact stays fixed on the object over time. The
robot can therefore still move the object along its trajectory
with different contacts, for example with the other hand or
2


with sliding fingers, which makes the manipulation unsta-
ble. DexDuet instead trains each robot hand to preserve the
human’s contacts, so that it stays on the part that the hu-
man’s hand holds without sliding.
Bimanual manipulation.
Two hands that manipulate one
object must coordinate, because each performs its own part
of the task and the task fails as soon as either one fails.
Imitation-learning policies for two robot arms build this co-
ordination into their architecture, for example with per-arm
modules that exchange information or predict a shared fu-
ture state [3, 12, 17, 20, 31, 49, 50, 56]. In reinforcement
learning of bimanual dexterous tasks, coordination has been
eased by treating each hand as a separate agent [4], by giv-
ing the two hands complementary roles [54], or by training
one hand before both [61]. Methods that transfer human
demonstrations to two dexterous hands have not used these
ideas. They either drive both hands with one policy, pos-
sibly on top of per-hand imitators [21, 30, 63], or learn an
independent policy for each hand [62]. With a single policy,
the learning of one hand is tied to that of the other, whereas
independent policies do not share what one hand learns with
the other. DexDuet lies between these two designs, inspired
by cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning [55] and
by decentralized agents that reason about their partner [13].
Each hand has its own policy, which observes the other
hand, and the two policies share one network, so that both
coordination and experience sharing are built into the pol-
icy.
Curricula for dexterous manipulation.
Learning dex-
terous manipulation from complex demonstrations is hard,
because a policy that starts at the first frame rarely reaches
the later phases.
Tracking methods therefore also start
episodes at later frames of the demonstration, either from
reference states [63] or from states the policy has already
reached [22, 52, 53], and some sample the start frames
more often where the policy fails [23].
A second tech-
nique assists the object, with a VOC [30, 63] or with relaxed
physics [21, 24], and reduces this assistance over training.
However, the assistance is reduced uniformly across the en-
tire demonstration, even though the difficulty usually lies in
only a few phases, so that progress on the whole demon-
stration is limited by its hardest phase. A-RSI instead sets
the assistance separately for each segment of the demonstra-
tion, lowering it when the episodes that start there succeed
and raising it when they fail, and starts episodes preferen-
tially in segments with high failure rates. A hard phase thus
keeps its assistance and receives more practice, and training
on the rest of the demonstration proceeds independently of
it.

3.4. Assisted Reference-State Initialization
Learning a complex bimanual demonstration is a hard ex-
ploration problem, because episodes rarely progress beyond
a difficult phase in the middle, such as a handover between
the hands. In motion imitation, reference-state initializa-
tion (RSI) [35] addresses this problem by starting episodes
from states sampled uniformly along the reference motion,
so that every phase is practiced. In dexterous retargeting, a
reference state consists of the hand configurations of the ref-
erence motion and the demonstrated object state of a frame.
RSI then faces two problems.
First, a reference state is
usually not physically valid, because the retargeted fingers
often leave a gap to the object or penetrate it. Without a
valid grasp, an object that starts in the air falls or is pushed
away before the policy can act. Second, uniform sampling
spends most episodes on phases that the policy has already
solved instead of on the difficult one. Assisted reference-
state initialization (A-RSI, Fig. 2c and Fig. S2) addresses
the first problem by assisting the object, which makes refer-
ence states usable, and by starting episodes from states that
the policy has reached. In addition, it addresses the second
problem by starting episodes more often where the policy
fails.
Per-segment assistance.
To make reference states usable,
we assist the object with the VOC [30], which pulls the ob-
ject toward its next demonstrated state xt+1 with a strength
set by its gains. Prior work [30, 63] uses one gain level for
the whole demonstration and lowers it only once the policy
succeeds over all of it, so a single hard phase stalls the cur-
riculum. We instead divide the demonstration into B seg-
ments of equal length, indexed by b, and give each segment
its own assistance level hb. This level sets the VOC gains of
every episode that starts in segment b, for the whole episode.
An episode succeeds if it reaches the end of the demonstra-
tion and fails if it is terminated early because its tracking
reward falls below a threshold. Each hb starts at a maxi-
mum hmax and is updated during training according to the

Under full assistance, an

episodes that start in this segment are then initialized from
its keyframes instead of reference states. A keyframe is
used only while the assistance of its segment is at least as
high as the level under which it was reached (Sec. A.5).

4. Experiments
Our experiments address four questions:
Q1 Does DexDuet track the object better than the com-
pared baselines for different benchmarks?
Q2 Does it preserve the human’s hand-object contacts?
Q3 Can the learned motions be executed on real robot
hands?
Q4 What do the per-hand policy, A-RSI and the interaction
rewards contribute, and at what training cost?
4.1. Experimental Setup
Benchmarks and baselines.
We evaluate on three dexter-
ous manipulation benchmarks and on a humanoid manipu-
lation challenge, each in the simulator of its baseline. The
DexMachina [30] and SPIDER [34] suites each have their
own seven ARCTIC [6] demonstrations in Genesis [10].
We evaluate DexMachina’s released checkpoints for all four
hands and rerun SPIDER’s official code, which optimizes
an open-loop control sequence instead of a policy. The Ma-
nipTrans suite [21] has ten OakInk2 [60] demonstrations in
Isaac Gym [29], and we retrain ManipTrans with its offi-
cial protocol. The V2D challenge [32] has 30 humanoid
episodes in MuJoCo Warp [11, 45], and we retrain its base-
line CHORD [63] with the challenge’s official PPO and
FlashSAC [18] recipes at the same budget of environment
steps as DexDuet (Sec. B).
Metrics.
We report the following four object-tracking
metrics: ADD-AUC [15, 30, 48], the mean per-point er-
ror of the object (MPPE), and the success rates SP-SR and
MP-SR. SP-SR uses the thresholds of SPIDER, and MP-SR
those of ManipTrans. These metrics evaluate only the first
requirement of Sec. 3.1, how closely the object follows its
demonstrated states. They do not reveal whether the robot
hands preserve the human’s hand-object contacts. We there-
fore introduce a further metric, Contact-AUC, which mea-
sures how well the constraint of Eq. (1) is met. For every
hold window w ∈W, it takes the contact drift ˆδw of the
corresponding robot hand and, as ADD-AUC does for the
object’s error, scores the share of thresholds τ between τmin

4.4. Ablation Studies
Q4: Contribution of each component.
Each component
prevents a different failure (Tab. 3 and Sec. D.4). With-
out the per-hand policy (e), tracking is less precise in 20
of the 21 runs paired by demonstration and seed. Replac-

until training stops (Fig. S3 and Tab. S20). Removing either
interaction reward (b, c) lowers Contact-AUC much more
than ADD-AUC, and most of this loss lies on windows in
which the object stays on track, so it comes from the con-
tacts (Tab. S7). Without both rewards (d), six of the 21 runs
fail.
5. Conclusion
Prior methods that learn bimanual dexterity from human
demonstrations mainly reward object tracking, and their
policies often track the object without preserving the hu-
man’s hand-object contacts. We presented DexDuet, which
preserves these contacts with two interaction rewards that
compare each robot hand with the human hand relative to
the object. To track complex demonstrations, DexDuet also
coordinates the two hands with per-hand policies and learns
the hard phases with its A-RSI curriculum. Across four
benchmarks and five robot embodiments, DexDuet tracks
the object and preserves the human’s contacts better than
prior methods, and its motions are executed on two real

A. Additional Method Details
Unless stated otherwise, the settings of this section apply to
two Inspire hands on ARCTIC demonstrations in Genesis.
The Allegro, XHand and Schunk hands keep the curricu-
lum of Tabs. S4 and S5 and the weights λhg and λhoc, and
Tab. S3 lists the settings that depend on the hand. Their
PD gains are those of DexMachina’s configuration of each
hand, and every reward term that averages over keypoints,
joints, links or action entries averages over those of the
hand. On the demonstrations of group A (Tab. S2), their re-
ward weights are those of DexMachina’s released runs with
the same hand, except that our Schunk runs keep the Inspire
value λbc = 0.3 instead of 0.2; no weight was tuned per
hand.
A.1. Observations and Actions
Object state.
The ARCTIC objects consist of two rigid
parts connected by a revolute joint.

A.3. PPO Details
The policy loss Lπ is the negative batch mean of the per-
hand surrogate of Eq. (5) [42].
Rewards are scaled by
0.1. For each hand, the advantage Ai
t is computed with
GAE [41] (γ = 0.99, λGAE = 0.95) from that hand’s value
head, and each hand’s advantages are normalized over the
whole batch. Both value heads regress the same shared re-
turn. The value loss is PPO’s clipped value loss on nor-
malized returns, averaged over samples and over the two
heads. The total loss is L = Lπ + 1
2cV LV + cbndLbnd with
cV = 4 and cbnd = 10−4, where the bound loss Lbnd pe-
nalizes action means outside [−1.1, 1.1]. There is no en-
tropy bonus. The clip range is ϵ = 0.2 for both the ratios
and the values. We use Adam with an initial learning rate
of 3 × 10−4. The rate is adapted after each pass over the
batch to keep the KL divergence near 0.008. It is divided by
1.5 when the KL exceeds 0.016 and multiplied by 1.5 when
the KL falls below 0.004, and it stays within [10−6, 10−2].
Gradients are clipped to norm 1. Each update collects 32
steps from 12,000 parallel environments (384,000 transi-
tions), and then makes 14 passes over the batch with mini-
batches of 96,000 transitions.

The challenge [32] uses 30 episodes from

B.2. DexDuet on the V2D Challenge
On the V2D challenge [32], a Unitree G1 humanoid repro-
duces a person moving an object, so its Dex3 hands are car-
ried by arms and by a body that has to stay balanced. Dex-
Duet keeps its three components on this robot and adapts
each of them to the whole body.
The observations stay
those of the challenge’s recipe, because the evaluator re-
quires their layout, and we also keep its termination and
PPO settings.
Policy.
The challenge’s recipe acts through a pre-trained
whole-body motion tracker [28], which turns the reference
motion into targets for the 29 joints of the legs, waist and
arms. The policy adds a correction to each of these targets
and to the reference targets of the 14 finger joints. Because
the arms position the hands, each per-hand policy controls
one arm and its hand, which makes 14 joints, and a third
policy controls the 15 joints of the legs and waist. The two
per-hand policies share their parameters. Unlike the floating
wrists on the tabletop, the arms of the G1 are mirror-image
joint chains on a common torso, and the challenge’s obser-
vations are expressed in the pelvis frame. We therefore re-
flect the right arm and hand across the sagittal plane of the
pelvis: positions, orientations and joint angles of its inputs
and outputs are mapped with the joint signs derived from
the URDF, so that the shared policy sees both sides in the
convention of the left. As the two hands do in Sec. 3.2, each
of the three policies observes the state and the latest joint
targets of the other two. As in Sec. 3.2, each has its own
probability ratio and value head, and all three receive the
same reward. The evaluator accepts a single fully connected
network, so after training we convert the three policies into
one network of that format and verify that it produces the
same actions.
Curriculum.
A-RSI replaces the challenge’s curriculum
and scales the challenge’s virtual object controller per seg-
ment, with segments of 25 frames.
An iteration of the
challenge’s PPO recipe collects 98,304 samples, against
384,000 for an update on the DexMachina suite, so we mul-
tiply A-RSI’s update counts by 3.9 to give each segment
the same number of samples between decisions. Keyframes
store the full simulator state of the humanoid and the ob-
ject, and a keyframe requires a mean tracking reward of at
least 0.6, against 0.5 on the DexMachina suite. Episodes
that start from a reference state in the middle of the demon-
stration keep the recipe’s 50-step settling phase, at the end
of which the assistance decays to the level of the segment,
while keyframe and first-frame starts begin at once. Train-
ing is capped at 20,000 iterations, the budget of the retrained
CHORD. DexDuet stops earlier on 10 of the 30 episodes,
a fixed number of iterations after its curriculum has with-
drawn all assistance.

B.3. Baselines
DexMachina is evaluated with the authors’ released
checkpoints.
SPIDER is rerun from its official code in our Genesis
scene with five seeds per demonstration. SPIDER optimizes
each demonstration separately. One run over the 200 frames
of a demonstration takes 1.4–19.9 min (median 7.9 min) on
one GPU, and the 35 runs take 5.8 h in total.
ManipTrans is retrained from scratch with its official
protocol in the scene with joints.
This protocol trains a
residual policy per demonstration on top of the released per-
hand imitators, with 4,096 environments, at most 10,000
iterations and early stopping after 1,000 iterations without
improvement. To check our ManipTrans setup, we repro-
duce its evaluation on the 77 OakInk2 validation sequences
with the Inspire hand, following the evaluation that the au-
thors describe in their code repository. We obtain success
rates of 57.8% on the 38 single-hand sequences and 32.9%
on the 39 bimanual ones, against 58.1% and 39.5% in the
paper [21]. On successful rollouts, our errors are within
0.06 cm and 0.3◦of the reported ones.
On the V2D challenge, we retrain CHORD with the chal-
lenge’s official code and both of its recipes for G1 + Dex3.
The PPO recipe keeps its settings and runs for 20,000 iter-
ations of 4,096 environments and 24 steps, or 1.97 billion
environment steps per episode. On episode 7, the simulator
occasionally returns a non-finite state that would end learn-
ing, so for this episode we reset the affected environment,
as our implementation does. The FlashSAC recipe is set for
0.35 billion environment steps, so we scale its settings that
depend on the budget, namely the total number of steps, the

stage boundaries of its curriculum and the checkpoint inter-
val, to the same 1.97 billion steps and keep everything else.
The challenge’s leaderboard also lists an entry of CHORD,
whose training budget is not published and whose policy is
not released (Tab. S16).
ConTrack [22] is rerun from its official code on the Dex-
Machina suite with XHand hands. We port its environment
to the scenes of DexMachina’s released XHand runs, with
the object not actuated, and keep its rewards, constraint han-
dling, PPO settings and network. It is trained with five seeds
per demonstration and DexDuet’s budget, namely 12,000
environments, 32 steps per update and the same maximum
number of updates per demonstration, without early stop-
ping, and, as in ConTrack, we evaluate its final checkpoint.
B.4. Training and Evaluation
Seeds and stopping.
On the DexMachina suite, DexDuet
is trained with five seeds per demonstration (15, 24, 42, 66
and 113), and the ablations of Tab. 3 with three (15, 42 and
113). On the SPIDER suite, the five seeds are 15, 24, 42,
43 and 66. On the ManipTrans suite, DexDuet and Ma-
nipTrans are both trained with three seeds (42, 24 and 66).
On V2D, every method is trained with one seed. On the
SPIDER suite, training uses 12,000 parallel environments.
On the SPIDER and ManipTrans suites, training takes at
most 15,000 updates, the assistance ceiling reaches zero at
update 10,000 (a deadline of 9,000 updates plus a 1,000-
update ramp), and training stops 1,500 updates after all as-
sistance is gone. All DexDuet runs that we train in Genesis,
including the ablations, use this stopping rule.
Evaluation.
Every method is evaluated from the first
frame of each demonstration, without assistance and with
deterministic actions. In Genesis, each policy is evaluated
on 100 parallel rollouts, with the first observation read from
the simulator after the reset. On the ManipTrans suite, ev-
ery policy is evaluated on 256 rollouts per demonstration,
which are the last two episodes of 128 environments. Ev-
ery rollout of both methods starts at the first frame of the
200-frame window, whereas ManipTrans’s own evaluation
starts up to 165 frames earlier on seven demonstrations.
ManipTrans’s evaluation is stochastic, so we fix its evalu-
ation seed, and over three evaluation seeds the standard de-
viation of its mean ADD-AUC and success rates is at most
0.8 points. On the V2D challenge, the official evaluator runs
one rollout per episode from the first frame. It is not deter-
ministic, so we report the mean of four runs.
Compute.
DexDuet trains each policy for 4,350–11,500
PPO updates, which are 1.67–4.42 B transitions on the Dex-
Machina and SPIDER suites and 0.58–1.51 B on the Manip-
Trans suite, and the ablation rows (b)–(g) stop after at most
13,000 updates (4.99 B transitions). On the DexMachina
suite, a typical run takes about one day on one GPU (me-
dian 21.7 h). ManipTrans trains only a residual policy on
top of its released, pre-trained per-hand imitators and stops
early, so its 2.1–4.9 h per run on the ManipTrans suite ex-
clude the training of the imitators.
B.5. Tracking Metrics
ADD-AUC.
We use DexMachina’s released code un-
changed. For each part, it samples 500 mesh vertices with
a fixed seed. For each rollout and frame, it computes the
mean distance between these vertices at the reached and at
the demonstrated pose. The accuracy at a threshold is the
share of (rollout, frame) pairs with an error below it, for
thresholds of 1, 2, . . . , 9 cm. The AUC is the trapezoidal
area under this accuracy curve, and the reported score is the
mean of the two parts’ AUCs. Following the released evalu-
ator, the state after step t is compared with frame t−1 of the
demonstration. On the SPIDER suite, we apply the same
definition to the saved object states of every method. We
sample 500 vertices per part with seed 42, use thresholds of
1, 2, . . . , 9 cm and average the AUCs of the two parts. The
moving part is placed by the joint angle. As in SPIDER’s
comparison with DexMachina, the state after each step is
compared with the frame of the demonstration that this step
tracks. On SPIDER’s released trajectories, this gives scores
close to those SPIDER reports (Sec. D). On the ManipTrans
suite, ADD-AUC is computed over each 200-frame win-
dow.
Success rates and MPPE.
In Tab. 1, SP-SR, MP-SR and
MPPE are computed with the evaluation code of the V2D
challenge on every benchmark. For each rollout, this code
averages the position error and the geodesic rotation error of
each object part over all frames, with the frame alignment
of ADD-AUC. SP-SR counts a rollout as a success if the
errors averaged over the parts are at most 10 cm and 0.5 rad,
which are the thresholds of SPIDER [34]. MP-SR counts it
as a success if the errors of every part are below 3 cm and
30◦, which are the object thresholds of ManipTrans [21].
MPPE places six points 5 cm from the origin of each part
along its axes and averages their distance over frames, parts
and rollouts. On the ManipTrans suite, rollouts that Manip-
Trans’s environment terminates early count as failures. For
MPPE, these rollouts keep their last pose, and those termi-
nated before the first recorded frame are left out (3 of 7,680
rollouts). The original criteria differ. SPIDER’s criterion
uses only the root part of the object. ManipTrans’s crite-
rion tests its object thresholds at every frame from frame
8 on. We report these original object criteria on their own
benchmarks (Sec. D).

C.2. Contact Points on Each Benchmark
On the DexMachina suite, the robot contacts ˆCw are the key-
points of hand i within 1.5 cm of part m at t that are closer
to m than to the other part. The keypoints of the Inspire
hand are its 18 tracked link frames, which are its 5 finger-
tips and 13 collision links (Tab. S3 for the other hands). For
SPIDER and ManipTrans, we set the recorded hand and ob-
ject states of their rollouts in our Genesis scene frame by
frame and read the keypoints there.
On the V2D challenge, the windows, the human contact
rule and the scoring stay the same. Each object is one rigid
part, and the human hands are SOMA meshes with 2,847
vertices per hand instead of MANO meshes. The links of
the Dex3 hand are 1.6 to 2.8 cm thick, and their origins lie
up to 2 cm from their surfaces. We therefore use 64 points
on the surface of each of its eight collision links, and the
robot contacts are the points within 5 mm of the object, the
distance at which a human contact is counted. The evaluator
saves no hand states, so we record them in four runs of it per
episode. Contact-AUC is averaged over the 29 episodes in
which the human hands touch the object. In the remaining
episode, the human hands never come within 5 mm of the
object. As on the other suites, contacts of the forearms, the
torso and the legs are not counted.
C.3. Where Contact-AUC Is Lost
Each window of W falls into one of three classes. A win-
dow is lost if the object is lost at t or t+∆. It has no contact
if the object is on track but hand i has no contact point on
part m at t. Otherwise it is touching, and its drift decides its
score. A run’s Contact-AUC is therefore the share of touch-
ing windows times the mean score over them. It is also the
share of windows with the object on track times the on-track
Contact-AUC, which is the mean score over these windows.
Table S7 gives this split for the ablations.
C.4. Contact-AUC at Matched Tracking
At equal tracking, DexDuet preserves the human’s contacts
better than DexMachina and than its ablation without both
interaction rewards. To separate the contacts from tracking,
we compare runs that track the object equally well. A pair
consists of two runs on the same hand and demonstration.
It is either one of DexDuet’s five seeds and one of Dex-
Machina’s five released checkpoints, or DexDuet (a) and an
ablation row with the same training seed. A pair is matched
if the two ADD-AUCs differ by at most 3 points. Table S8
lists the matched pairs, and Fig. S4 shows two of them.
On the DexMachina suite, both methods track the ob-
ject at about 94 ADD-AUC in the matched pairs. In the
median over pairs, DexDuet’s Contact-AUC is higher in ev-
ery hand and demonstration that has a matched pair. The
matched gap comes both from windows in which Dex-
Machina’s hand is not on the part that the human holds and
from windows in which its hand touches the part but its con-
tacts slide.
The ablation pairs keep the training seed and the demon-
stration fixed.
At matched tracking, removing the hand
graph reward (b) lowers Contact-AUC in 10 of 13 pairs, and
removing both rewards (d) lowers it in all 9.

D.3. Holding After the Last Frame
On the DexMachina suite, we re-simulate each policy from
the simulator states of its own rollout and let it keep acting
for 3 s after the last frame, with every reference frozen at
the last frame. A rollout holds the object if the object stays
within 5 cm of its final pose for the whole 3 s, and drops it
if the object ends the 3 s more than 10 cm below its final
pose or off the table. We run it with all four hands on the
checkpoints of Tab. 1. DexDuet holds the object in more
runs and drops it in fewer runs than DexMachina with every
hand (Tab. S18).
D.4. Ablations
Each row of Tab. 3 removes or replaces components of
DexDuet and keeps everything else, including the reward
weights of Tab. S2 and the settings of Tab. S5.
Variants.
Rows (b) and (c) set λhg or λhoc to zero, and
row (d) sets both to zero. Row (e) replaces the two per-
hand policies by the unified policy of DexMachina [30], an
MLP with hidden layers of 512, 512, 256 and 128 units
shared by the actor and the critic. It maps the observation
of both hands to their concatenated 24-dimensional action,
with one value head and one PPO ratio for the joint action.
Rows (f) and (g) replace A-RSI by DexMachina’s single-
level VOC curriculum (Sec. A.5), starting from row (d) and
from DexDuet, respectively.
Training and evaluation.
Every row is trained with seeds
15, 42 and 113 under the stopping rule of Sec. B.4 and the
update caps of Tab. S5. Table S19 adds the other metrics,
the comparison with row (a) paired by demonstration and
seed, and the share of rollouts that hold the object after the
last frame, and Tab. S20 gives each demonstration.
Interaction rewards.
The interaction rewards act mainly
on the contacts. Removing the hand graph reward or the
hand-object contact reward lowers Contact-AUC by 9.0 and
15.1 points, against 2.2 and 3.3 points of ADD-AUC. The
two rewards matter most on different demonstrations. With-
out the hand graph reward, Notebook-300 loses the most,
12.7 points of ADD-AUC and 33.7 points of Contact-AUC,
and without the hand-object contact reward, all three seeds
track the bottle poorly on Ketchup-100. Removing both
(d) makes training less reliable: six of its 21 runs fail SP-
SR, two each on Ketchup-100 and Waffleiron-300 and one
each on Ketchup-300 and Notebook-300, against one run of
DexDuet (Tab. S19). On the five demonstrations on which
(f), the variant without the interaction rewards and A-RSI,
tracks the object better than DexDuet, by 1.3 to 3.3 points of
ADD-AUC, DexDuet preserves the human’s contacts better
by 16 to 25 points of Contact-AUC.
Per-hand policy.
The unified policy (e) is below Dex-
Duet in both metrics in every paired run on six of the seven
demonstrations.
A-RSI.
A-RSI matters most where the single assistance
level stalls. On Ketchup-300, all six runs of (f) and (g) reach
the update cap of 13,000 and end with a lower ADD-AUC
and Contact-AUC than their counterparts with A-RSI, (d)
and (a). With the interaction rewards, row (a) reaches a
higher Contact-AUC than (g) on six of the seven demon-
strations.
D.5. Real-World Replays
Figures S7 and S8 show the replays of Sec. 4.3 frame by

E. Limitations
Like DexMachina and ManipTrans, DexDuet trains one
policy per demonstration, which takes about a day on one
GPU, and it does not generalize to new objects or motions.
The policy observes the object state of the simulator, and
the demonstration must provide accurate hand and object
motion. On the real robots, we replay simulated rollouts
open-loop (Sec. 4.3), and closed-loop deployment with an
estimated object state is left to future work.
