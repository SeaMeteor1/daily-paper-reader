Abstract
Collaboration promises to extend humanoid capabilities be-
yond what individual robots can achieve. However, realiz-
ing this potential requires coordinated whole-body interac-
tions, coherent long-horizon planning, and precise synchro-
nization across robots. We introduce HEAVE-HO, a decen-
tralized framework for long-horizon collaborative multi-
humanoid loco-manipulation. At the low level, we develop
a multi-agent post-training framework that adapts a hu-
manoid foundation tracking model to physically coupled
interactions between humanoids, enabling coordinated de-
centralized control from local observations alone. At the
high level, we train a vision-language planner and design a
speech-based leader–follower coordination mechanism to
synchronize decentralized execution without network com-
munication.
Trained with two identical humanoids, our
system scales to teams of up to ten robots and general-
izes to heterogeneous teams without retraining. To bench-
mark these capabilities, we introduce HH-BENCH, com-
prising 12 tasks involving teams of 2–10 humanoids, in-
cluding heterogeneous teams. Systematic evaluations show
that HEAVE-HO consistently outperforms baseline meth-
ods, while real-world experiments demonstrate robust de-
centralized collaboration across challenging tasks.
1. Introduction
Learning-based whole-body control has made individual
humanoids increasingly capable [27, 42, 77, 78]. However,
many tasks in homes, warehouses, and construction sites
remain beyond the physical capabilities of a single robot.
Handling large or heavy objects, such as tables, bed frames,
and large boxes, often requires multiple humanoids to share
the load and coordinate their motion. Collaboration there-
fore promises to overcome these physical limits.
Realizing this promise involves three interdependent
challenges.
First, interaction is tightly coupled: forces
transmitted through a shared object make each robot’s bal-

long-horizon tasks require visually grounded planning of
coordinated action sequences, such as reorienting the team
and its shared load before passing through a narrow door-
way. Third, execution must be synchronized: a mistimed
lift or step can destabilize the shared load and the robots.
These challenges become especially demanding under
decentralized execution. A centralized policy can use team-
wide observations to jointly coordinate the robots’ actions
and align their execution timing [9, 44, 45, 62]. However,
its input context and joint action space grow with team
size, so policies with fixed input and output dimensions
require modification and often retraining when robots are
added. Gathering team-wide observations also requires net-
work communication at inference. A shared decentralized
policy can instead preserve the same per-robot observation
and action interface as the team grows, but must coordinate
from local observations alone. Our goal is to combine this
scalability with tightly synchronized whole-body collabora-
tion, without network communication.
We introduce HEAVE-HO,
a decentralized frame-
work for long-horizon collaborative multi-humanoid loco-
manipulation.
At the low level, our multi-agent post-
training framework augments a frozen humanoid founda-
tion tracking model [42] with a residual policy shared across
robots. We train this policy with multi-agent reinforcement
learning using a centralized critic and shared actor, while
each robot relies only on local observations at execution.
The resulting controller executes lifting, stepping, and turn-
ing skills with compatible start and end configurations, en-
abling their composition into long-horizon behaviors. At
the high level, we train a vision-language planner to select
the next team skill from a leader robot’s visual observations.
A speech-based leader–follower mechanism aligns skill de-
cisions and execution timing without network communica-
tion: the leader announces a skill, followers recognize it
locally, and all robots begin on a shared execution sched-
ule. The leader role can be assigned to any robot in the
team, and the same speech interface allows a person to fol-
low commands or act as the leader.
To evaluate these capabilities,
we introduce HH-
BENCH, a benchmark of 12 collaborative tasks for teams of
2–10 humanoids, including heterogeneous teams. The tasks
span lifting and carrying, shoulder carrying, pulling, push-
ing, handover, and precise placement under spatial con-
straints such as narrow doors and corridors.
Collabora-
tive skills and task stages can be chained into long-horizon
tasks. Across these tasks, our low-level policy succeeds in
94% of simulated episodes, compared with at most 29%
for baseline and ablated controllers.
Our planner com-
pletes 43 of 50 long-horizon trials, compared with 21 for
zero-shot GPT-6 (Astra), while reducing per-decision la-
tency by a factor of about 120. Without retraining, the pol-
icy learned with two identical humanoids scales to teams
of up to ten robots and collaborates with a different hu-
manoid platform and with people. Extensive real-world ex-
periments further demonstrate robust decentralized collab-
oration on long-horizon tasks. Videos of the real-world and
simulated experiments are provided on the project page.
In summary, our contributions are:
• A multi-agent post-training framework that adapts a
frozen humanoid foundation tracking model to physically
coupled collaboration through a shared residual policy,
for decentralized control from local observations.
• A trained vision-language model for coherent long-
horizon planning, together with a speech-based leader–
follower coordination mechanism for synchronized de-
centralized execution without network communication.
• HH-BENCH, a benchmark of 12 collaborative tasks for
teams of 2–10 humanoids, accompanied by systematic
evaluations of scalability, heterogeneous collaboration,
and real-world deployment.
2. Related Work
Humanoid whole-body control and loco-manipulation.
Learning-based methods have substantially advanced hu-
manoid locomotion and athletic control [23, 35, 54, 55,
68, 78, 80]. To broaden these capabilities, motion imita-
tion provides a scalable route to acquiring diverse whole-
body skills from human demonstrations [10, 11, 20, 25–
28, 32, 38, 74, 75].
SONIC [42] further scales motion
tracking into a generalist controller with a unified interface
for teleoperation and general tracking.
Recent work ex-
tends motion tracking to contact-rich interaction using mo-
tion capture data [47, 70, 77] or human videos [2, 65, 67].
Despite this progress, these methods primarily focus on
the capabilities of individual humanoids. Our work adapts
a frozen foundation tracking model [42] through multi-
agent residual learning, enabling physically coupled multi-
humanoid loco-manipulation collaboration with decentral-
ized control from local observations alone.
Centralized and decentralized multi-robot collabora-
tion.
Centralized policies jointly process team observa-
tions and predict actions for multiple robots, support-
ing multi-arm manipulation [63, 64], collaborative legged
pushing [46], and collaboration among heterogeneous
robots [79].
Decentralized approaches learn coordinated
policies through multi-agent reinforcement learning with
centralized training and decentralized execution [41, 72] or
joint imitation learning with diffusion policies and consen-
sus representations [15, 24]. CHORUS [16] further extends
this direction to a shared pretrained vision-language-action
model, enabling heterogeneous mobile manipulators to act
independently from their own observations. Closely related
to our setting, decPLM [50] learns decentralized pinch–
2


lift–move control for quadruped–arm teams, assuming ex-
ternally specified contact locations, synchronized initiation,
and payload motion commands. In humanoid settings, ex-
isting work primarily investigates collaboration with human
partners [4, 18, 36, 76]. Cooperative manipulation among
multiple simulated characters has also been explored, which
is confined to simulation only [21, 39, 71]. Our work targets
autonomous long-horizon collaborative loco-manipulation
by real humanoid teams, combining decentralized whole-
body control with visually grounded skill planning and syn-
chronized execution without network communication.
Vision-language task planning.
Language and vision-
language models support long-horizon robot tasks by se-
lecting and composing skills [1, 17, 30, 31] or generating
executable control programs [37]. At the execution level,
vision-language-action models map visual observations and
language instructions to robot actions [5–7, 22, 34, 48, 53,
66]. Hierarchical frameworks connect these levels by cou-
pling task reasoning with learned visuomotor policies [60]
or humanoid motion and skill controllers [14, 69, 73]. For
robot teams, centralized language planners allocate tasks
using team-level information [33, 40], while decentralized
and hybrid planning frameworks coordinate robot-specific
agents through explicit inter-agent dialogue [9, 44], which
need network communication. In contrast, HEAVE-HO co-
ordinates physically coupled humanoids without inter-robot
network communication: a trained vision-language plan-
ner selects shared team skills from the leader’s local visual
observations, and speech-based coordination synchronizes
their execution. A shared low-level policy realizes each skill
through decentralized whole-body control using only each
robot’s local observations.
3. Method
3.1. Overview
We consider a team of N humanoids manipulating a shared
object. We call the team decentralized in a strict sense: ev-
ery robot acts only on its own observations, and no network
message is exchanged between robots during a task. The
low-level policy runs on each robot’s on-board computer.
Agent i therefore observes only its proprioception, a short
window of its own future reference motion, and the object
specification c = (L, W, H, m) (length, width, height, and
mass); it has no access to its partners’ states, and their ac-
tions affect it only through forces transmitted by the shared
object.
HEAVE-HO operates at two levels (Fig. 2). The two lev-
els communicate through a vocabulary of team skills K,
such as lift, put down, a step in each direction, and a rota-
tion in place. The high level decides which team skill comes
next, every few seconds, and makes sure that all robots start
it together. The low level turns the current team skill into
whole-body motion on every robot at 50 Hz. Each robot
maps a team skill to its own frame through a fixed role map
ρi : K →K; for two robots facing each other across a box,
moving the team toward the leader’s front is a forward step
for the leader and a backward step for its partner, whereas
lifting and rotating are the same for both.
3.2. Low Level: Multi-Agent Post-Training
Composable team skills.
Every team skill starts and ends
at one of two static hub poses: S0, standing empty-handed,
and S1, standing while holding the object. Lift moves the
team from S0 to S1, put down returns it to S0, and steps and
rotations keep it at S1. Because all skills share these end-
points, any skill can follow any other, and a long-horizon
behavior reduces to a sequence of discrete choices. Skills
are defined at the team level: all robots start and end every
skill in one canonical team layout and follow one common
displacement profile during a move, so the spacing between
partners stays constant over long sequences. Steps have a
fixed length and rotations a fixed angle, so the planner can
reason about distance in units of skills, and when no new
skill arrives the team simply holds its hub pose.
Reference motions.
We obtain references from two
sources. We record pairs of people performing the task, re-
cover their motion from video [59], and retarget it to the
robots; we also generate references procedurally with in-
verse kinematics, which adds objects and interaction modes
without new recordings. All references are cut into hub-to-
hub skills and conditioned on c: hand spread, grasp height,
squat depth, and partner spacing adapt to the object, while
the leg motion of each step is shared across objects.
Residual post-training.
Learning balance and locomo-
tion from scratch under multi-agent coupling is sample-
inefficient and fragile.
We freeze a foundation tracking
model [42] as a base policy πbase, which maps propriocep-
tion and reference to joint targets, and learn a residual policy
πθ that observes and corrects the base action:

Training for composition and generalization.
The skill
is encoded only in the reference, so one network executes
every skill and transitions never switch controllers. To cover
all transitions, we train on long random chains of legal
skills with balanced directions and random waiting times at
the hubs, and initialize episodes at random points of these
chains [51]. The object specification c is an explicit input;
during training the true size varies by ±10% and the mass
by ±20% around the nominal value given to the policy, so a
single network adapts its grasp and posture to different ob-
jects. We further randomize friction, robot properties, and
external pushes, and apply curricula on actuation delay and
on the object tracking tolerance [52, 61].
3.3. High Level: Planning and Synchronization
Vision-language planner.
A leader decides the next team
skill with a vision-language model. Given the leader’s head-
camera image and the task instruction, the model outputs
one token from K ∪{HOLD, DONE}. It is queried after each
skill completes, so every decision is made from a view of
the team at rest, and a long-horizon task unfolds as a se-
quence of such decisions (Fig. 3). We fine-tune Qwen3-VL-
2B [3] with LoRA [29] (settings in App. D.1) on episodes
in which the leader’s image is recorded whenever a team
skill is committed. In the real world, an operator selects the
skills through the same interface, and we collect 24 episodes
for each task of Sec. 5.3; in simulation, a scripted planner
selects them, and we collect 1,000 episodes for each task
with initial positions sampled over a wide range. A single
decision maker gives the team one coherent plan, which in-
dependent planners on each robot cannot guarantee. The
planner can run on any computer that only the leader is
connected to, since it never exchanges information with the
other robots, and it is optional: without it, a person can act
as the leader.
Speech-based leader-follower synchronization.
Start-
ing a skill together is critical: a late lift tilts the object, and
a lone step pulls a partner off balance. We separate what the
team does from when it does it. What is decided once, by
the leader. When is fixed by a shared skill schedule: a skill
may only start on a coarse grid of control ticks, so robots
that learn about the same skill at slightly different moments
still start it together. The decision is a single discrete to-
ken, which the leader speaks through its speaker; every fol-
lower recognizes it with its on-board microphone, and every
robot schedules the skill on the next slot of the grid, with the
leader offsetting its own start by the expected acoustic de-
lay. Because the grid is coarser than the variation in acoustic
delay, this variation does not change the start slot, and the
team starts every skill together without exchanging a single
packet. The leader is a role rather than a fixed robot, since
every robot can host the same planner, and speech lets peo-
ple join the team: a person can follow the spoken skills or
take over as leader.

4. HH-BENCH: Multi-Humanoid Benchmark
Evaluations of humanoid loco-manipulation mostly con-
sider a single robot, and multi-agent benchmarks [8, 57]
rarely involve whole-body contact with a shared object.
There is thus no common testbed for humanoid teams that
are physically coupled through a shared object, where the
balance and grasp of each robot depend on its partners. HH-
BENCH collects 12 collaborative tasks in which a team of
humanoids must act on the same object at the same time, of-
ten over long sequences of coordinated skills. The tasks dif-
fer in how the robots interact with the object (lifting and car-
rying, carrying on the shoulder, pulling, pushing, handing
over, and precise placement) and in how the environment
constrains the motion (Fig. 4; App. B.1 lists all tasks). No
task prescribes a team: each can be performed by teams of
different sizes, including people and other humanoid plat-
forms, in simulation or in the real world. Together they
test the three requirements of collaboration: tightly cou-
pled whole-body interaction, coherent long-horizon plan-
ning, and synchronized execution.
Constrained transport.
The narrow door is the signature
task: the door is narrower than the object along its carrying
direction, so the team first rotates the object in place and
then walks through, with one robot moving forward and its
partner backward. A team that only walks along a fixed
direction cannot solve it. In the L-shaped corridor the team
combines straight and sideways steps and must decide when
to change direction while the robots and the object stay clear
of the walls. Carrying an L-shaped sofa onto a target area
marked on the floor adds an irregular object whose load
is distributed unevenly between the robots. Precise place-
ment requires the team to place a box within a target region,
which calls for the richest set of gaits: walking forward,
backward, and sideways, and turning in place.
Team scaling and heterogeneity.
The table carry suits
larger teams: a long table leaves room for many robots
along its edges. Any robot can be replaced by a person who
follows the spoken skills, or by a humanoid with a different
body; Secs. 5.2 and 5.5 report the teams we evaluate.
Diverse interaction modes.
Beyond lifting with the
hands, the benchmark includes carrying a palanquin or a
steel bar on the shoulders, pulling a fire hose, pushing a box
or a millstone, and handing a box down from a robot on a
higher level to a partner below, who must take over the load
at a different height. In our setup, the higher level is the
bed of a truck, and the robot holding the box starts at differ-
ent depths inside the truck and must first walk to its edge.
These tasks change where the contacts are and how force is
shared, while all robots must still act together.
Composing long-horizon tasks.
Because every task is
executed as a sequence of hub-to-hub team skills, tasks can
be chained into longer ones. For example, two robots carry
a box up a flight of stairs onto an elevated platform and
place it on a wheeled dolly; one of them then leaves its part-
ner, walks to the edge of the platform, and hands a second
box of a different size down to a third robot standing below.
Such compositions test whether the team stays coordinated
across stages that use different skills, and whether a robot
can leave one team and join another within one task.
Inputs and evaluation.
Robots use only on-board sens-
ing and the nominal object specification; motion capture
and network communication between robots are not al-
lowed. We report the success rate over 10 trials per task
for real-world experiments and planners, and over three
seeds of 512 teams for low-level controllers in simulation
(Sec. 5.4). In simulation, the initial positions of the robots
and the object are sampled over a wide range that covers
both easy and hard configurations, whereas real-world trials
start from a nominal configuration with small perturbations.
Success rates in simulation and in the real world are there-
fore not directly comparable. A trial succeeds if the team
completes the task and reaches the target location without
dropping the object.
5. Experiments
We evaluate HEAVE-HO on HH-BENCH and answer four
questions. (Q1) Can decentralized humanoid teams com-
plete long-horizon collaborative tasks in the real world
without communication? (Q2) Does the high-level planner
choose better team skills than alternative decision makers?
(Q3) How does the post-trained low-level policy compare
with baseline controllers, and which of its design choices
matter? (Q4) Does a policy trained with two identical hu-
manoids generalize to larger and heterogeneous teams?

5.1. Setup
We use Unitree G1 humanoids with a head-mounted stereo
camera, and an EngineAI PM01 for heterogeneous teams.
The PM01 runs its own policy trained with the same recipe.
Policies run on each robot’s on-board computer at 50 Hz and
are trained in Isaac-based simulation [43, 49] with 4,096
parallel two-robot environments [56].
5.2. Real-World Collaboration
Figure 5 shows real-world experiments of HEAVE-HO. Ev-
ery robot runs the shared low-level policy on its own on-
board computer, and no network message is exchanged be-
tween robots during a task. Two-robot teams pass through
the narrow door, follow the L-shaped corridor, hand a box
down from a truck, and place a box within a target region. A
person can also take the place of one robot, carrying a box
through the narrow door or up a flight of stairs together with
a G1. Finally, the two-robot policy is deployed to larger and
heterogeneous teams that carry a table (Sec. 5.5). A person
on the team can also take over as the leader and speak the
team skills, as we do in some of these experiments. Ev-
ery skill must start on all partners together, since a late lift
tilts the object and a lone step pulls a partner off balance.
The videos on the project page show the spoken commands
and the synchronized start of each skill. Across all tasks,
the teams succeed in 92 of 100 trials (App. C.1 lists every
team). Stair carry is the hardest (7/10): each step leaves
room for little more than one foot, so small errors in foot
placement are not tolerated.
5.3. High-Level Planning
We evaluate the full system with different decision makers
on five long-horizon tasks, with 10 trials per task (Tab. 1
and Fig. 6a). Two run in the real world: the narrow door
and the L-shaped corridor. Three run in simulation: a box
handover from the bed of a truck, in which the robot holding
the box starts at different depths inside the truck and must
walk to its edge before handing the box down; carrying a
long table with four robots to the front of a sofa; and plac-
ing an L-shaped sofa on a target area marked on the floor.
We compare our fine-tuned Qwen3-VL-2B planner against
two strong baselines: (1) zero-shot GPT-6 (Astra) with the
same skill interface, and (2) CHORUS [16], a decentralized
policy executed independently on each robot. We fine-tune
CHORUS on the same data following its recipe, including
the π0.5 backbone [53], LoRA adapters, and a fixed iden-
tity prompt per robot, and change only its output: instead of
action chunks, it selects team skills like our planner, which
isolates the coordination problem. An operator who selects
the team skills manually serves as the upper bound.
Our planner matches the operator on all three simulated
tasks and approaches it on the two real-world tasks, whereas
GPT-6 (Astra) used zero-shot fails far more often on every

5.5. Train with Two, Deploy with Many
All G1 policies are trained with exactly two identical robots.
We deploy the same policy, without retraining, to teams that
differ from this configuration in size and in composition. In
the real world (Fig. 5), four G1 robots carry a table as two
pairs side by side, three G1 robots carry it with a person, a
G1 carries it with a person and a PM01, and a PM01 carries
it with a person; these teams succeed in 37 of 40 trials. In
simulation, the same policy drives teams of four, eight, and
ten G1 robots carrying a table (Fig. 7). Two properties make
this possible: the shared actor has no notion of team size,
and partners affect each robot only through the object. Pa-
rameter sharing is essential, since independent actors would
require a new actor for every additional robot.
6. Conclusion
We
introduced
HEAVE-HO,
a
decentralized
frame-
work for long-horizon collaborative multi-humanoid loco-
manipulation. At the low level, multi-agent post-training
turns a frozen humanoid foundation tracking model into a
shared controller for physically coupled whole-body inter-
8


action. At the high level, a vision-language planner gives
the team one coherent long-horizon plan, more accurately
and about 120 times faster than GPT-6 (Astra) used zero-
shot, and a speech-based leader-follower mechanism exe-
cutes it in synchrony without network communication. On
HH-BENCH, HEAVE-HO completes long-horizon collab-
orative tasks in the real world, and a policy trained with
two identical humanoids carries over to teams of up to ten
robots, to a different humanoid platform, and to people.
Limitations.
Synchronization relies on followers hearing
the leader, so it requires a reasonably quiet environment and
reliable sound pickup. Training the planner for a new task
requires demonstrations collected manually by teleopera-
tion, which is time-consuming.

D.2. Low-Level Policy Training
We post-train the low-level policy in Isaac Lab with teams
of two Unitree G1 robots. A shared actor predicts joint-
target residuals on top of frozen SONIC, and a centralized
critic sees the concatenated observations of both robots dur-
ing training. We train a teacher with privileged simulator
observations and distill its mean actions into a student while
training an adaptation module to predict the privileged la-
tent from local inputs. We then fine-tune the student with
MAPPO in closed loop. Both teacher and student use the
same frozen SONIC base. Table 6 gives the MAPPO set-
tings.
For the box-carrying skills (lifting, putting down,
stepping in each direction, and turning in place), which to-
gether cover the two-robot box-carrying tasks such as the
narrow door and the L-shaped corridor, and for the box han-
dover, training uses 4,096 parallel teams and a budget of 109
environment steps per training stage, counting one step per
team.
