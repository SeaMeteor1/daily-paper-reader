Abstract
General-purpose behavior models increasingly internalize broad procedural knowl-
edge during training, making their adaptation to new goals and skill interfaces
less a problem of learning entirely new behaviors than of reconfiguring existing
competence. We study this capability as reconfigurability, the ability to realize
new behaviors by cost-efficiently restructuring existing components and relations
under sparse governing information, where task specifications provide high-level
goals and procedure specifications expose a limited set of reusable factors, leaving
the concrete bindings and compositions required for execution underdetermined.
We propose RecPo, a framework that represents skills task-agnostically through
multiple procedure views and materializes executable skill sequences by compos-
ing stored transitions to realize task-specific changes. RecPo maintains a procedure
knowledge base of skill-induced transitions, with each view exposing a reusable
factor and preserving local skill connectivity. At deployment, it interprets task
specifications through the same views and resolves the resulting compositional
queries over the stored relations to bind skills for execution. On long-horizon
tasks in an extended Franka Kitchen benchmark, RecPo achieves 91.9% ordered
completion with 1.0 ms of task inference per episode across varying procedure
support and task-record sparsity. Real-robot studies further demonstrate adaptation
through local memory edits without task-specific retraining.

Introduction
Reconfigurability is a core systems-level capability for lifelong embodied agents deployed in open-
ended physical environments, enabling cost-effective adaptation under the constrained computational
and physical resources available on-site [1–3]. Since design-time knowledge cannot cover every
case [4] and runtime exposes only a partial and shifting subset of deployment conditions, agents must
locally update existing competence as user specifications over behaviors and tasks change. These
limitations compound for embodied agents, whose deployments take place in physical environments
where spatial layouts, object properties, and physical dynamics vary across sites and shift over time.
Modern Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models [5–9] consolidate broad procedural knowledge
at training time, leaving deployment to local adaptation. Performing such adaptation in practice,
however, remains difficult: full retraining is costly and prone to catastrophic forgetting [10, 11], while
ad hoc conditioning offers only transient adjustments [12]. Therefore, reconfiguration is not only
about adaptation but also about externalizing this procedural knowledge into a form governable
through its environment-grounded effects, to enable cost-effective updates at deployment [13–17].
Recent efforts toward reconfigurability increasingly rely on external memories of prior behaviors, in
which adaptation involves adding, removing, or reweighting components rather than updating model

parameters, giving rise to two complementary lines of research. Behavior retrieval organizes prior
experiences as independently accessible records, either by retrieving trajectories [18–27] or by routing
over learned skill embeddings and prototypes [28–33], allowing individual components to be added
or removed without restructuring the entire memory. Structured procedural representations [34–37]
instead encode explicit connectivity among reusable behavior components, supporting compositional
access where a target instruction or goal is addressed by combining connected components.
However, these approaches face distinct challenges under sparse governing information, where task
goals, reusable procedural factors, and prior experiences provide fragmented evidence that does not
enumerate their possible task-specific combinations. Under these conditions, task demonstrations
provide sparse snapshots of desired state changes, while procedure records capture skill-induced
transitions realizable by the underlying behavior model without specifying how to compose them
for execution. (i) Behavior retrieval typically matches stored experiences independently, leaving
the transition compatibility between retrieved records under-specified. (ii) Structured procedural
approaches explicitly represent transition connectivity, but often encode it within a single state or skill
abstraction, which can obscure factor-specific transition relations. Reconfiguration of robot behavior
therefore requires a structure that preserves factor-specific transition connectivity across varying
contexts and supports task-conditioned composition of executable skill paths.
To address this gap, we propose RecPo, a multi-view update-and-compose framework for reconfig-
urable skill policies that maintains editable procedure and task memories to materialize task-specific
executable skill sequences. In RecPo, the Procedure Knowledge Base organizes task-agnostic skill-
induced transition records into view-local graphs, preserving skill connectivity and effect evidence
across reusable factors of variation. At test time, Task Materialization uses these views to abstract the
task demonstration into a compositional query of supported view-level changes, then resolves the
query into an executable skill sequence by tracing compatible transitions across stored graphs.
We evaluate RecPo on an extended Franka Kitchen benchmark with held-out long-horizon tasks
requiring compositions beyond stored skill records. By varying skill-context diversity, view reso-
lution, and task-demonstration waypoint gaps, we assess reconfiguration under sparse procedure
and task evidence. RecPo achieves 91.9% ordered completion at 1.0 ms of task inference per
episode, outperforming retrieval and skill-chaining baselines. Real-robot studies further demonstrate
deployment-time adaptation through local memory updates.
Our contributions are as follows:
• We formulate reconfigurable skill policy under sparse governing information as a cost-aware
update-and-compose problem over editable procedure and task memories.
• We propose RecPo, a framework that composes task-agnostic skill records into task-specific
executable skill sequences under a supplied procedure-view interface.
• We design a controlled protocol on an extended Franka Kitchen isolating procedure- and task-side
sparseness, with a real-robot study of deployment-time adaptation.

Related Work
Memory-based policy adaptation in robot learning. Recent robot-learning systems increasingly
treat memory as an external mechanism for cost-effective policy adaptation, rather than only as hidden
state or model parameters. One line retrieves relevant demonstrations or experiences at deployment
time and executes them through replay, alignment, or visual correspondence [38–40]. Another line
uses retrieval to curate, fuse, or bootstrap task-relevant data from large-scale experience, improving
few-shot generalization and robustness [18–27]. Recent robot policies further connect memory with
in-context adaptation, retrieving task-relevant keyframes or sub-trajectories as context for VLA
controllers and decision-transformer policies [14, 41, 42]. These in-context uses bring memory closer
to policy execution, but still leave procedural recomposition inside the retrieved context or policy
behavior rather than exposing it as a memory operation. This makes changes in object bindings, goal
orders, contexts, or skill granularities difficult to update, inspect, and measure at test time.
Routing-based policy reconfiguration. In lifelong learning, such retrieval capabilities integrate
external memory into learning and decision-making, separating generalized skill knowledge in pre-
trained policies from task-specific composition delegated to test-time routing [11]. These approaches
treat memory as support for selecting behaviors, routing over an expandable set of expert modules
via similarity matching [28–31], merging retrieved skills into connected behaviors [33], or matching
trajectory distributions at runtime to reconcile modules trained at different stages [32]. However,
collapsing diverse factors of variation into a single embedding space coarsens the state abstraction,
and routing it from sparse governing information limits forward transfer [43, 44]. This motivates
decomposing behavior memory along partial views with local connectivity within each view.
Structured procedural representations. A complementary line exposes connectivity among reusable
behaviors explicitly. Rooted in the options framework [45], these methods chain temporally extended
primitives through their initiation and termination conditions to form explicit procedural structure [46–
48]. A parallel direction composes options through learned models or weighted combinations of
primitives [49–51]. Building on this connectivity view, recent works organize behavior memory
as graphs over states, transitions, or skills and search them for temporal connectivity, including
deep skill graphs over discovered options [48, 52], graph-based retrieval and stitching for goal-
conditioned imitation [34, 35], and skill composition over learned operators [36, 37]. However, this
connectivity is built along a single axis, typically temporal adjacency, and is fused with its underlying
representation, leaving no handle to reorganize the same records under sparse task evidence. We
propose RecPo, which stores skill-induced records under multiple partial views with local connectivity
and materializes an executable structure across views when a task arrives.

5
Methods
Following the update-and-compose formulation, RecPo instantiates (2) through two named memory
interfaces that separate procedure storage from task-conditioned composition. Across these interfaces,
multiple procedure views preserve distinct state equivalences over the same skill-induced transitions,
allowing task materialization to combine task-relevant distinctions into a task-specific abstraction.
(i) Procedure knowledge base. The Procedure Knowledge Base realizes the procedure-memory
pair (Kp
Ω, UΩ). It updates and stores skill-induced transition evidence as procedure records, reads
them through partial procedure views, and organizes the resulting view-projected records into skill-
transition graphs and correlation-marked records. This yields a persistent procedure substrate whose
reusable executable transitions support later reconfiguration.
(ii) Task materialization. The task-memory pair (Kp
τ, UT ) maintains current task-state evidence.
The composition map ϕ is realized by Task Materialization, which folds this evidence into the same
canonical view-node space, forms task-query abstractions, and completes the induced query over the
procedure substrate as current-state-conditioned skill paths. The resulting executable approximation
of the task quotient carries prefix states and masks for grounding and local rematerialization during
execution. Figure 2 provides an overview.

Experiments
Our experiments evaluate whether RecPo can reconfigure policies under sparse governing informa-
tion, manifesting in procedure specifications that under-determine how to compose stored skills, and
in task specifications that under-determine which composition to produce. We additionally examine
whether these gains transfer to a real robot. We structure the evaluation around three questions:

6.1
Experiment Settings
Environment. We build on Franka Kitchen [58, 59] by extending it with a broader set of causally
independent [60] subtasks that admit varied executions, expanding the space of combinatorial long-
horizon compositions and the diversity of contexts in which each skill is observed. We define
realization length as the number of subtasks executed in sequence, and extend the original 24 length-4
realizations of Franka Kitchen up to length-7.
Evaluation protocol. We design the evaluation to stress-test the update-and-compose setting under
sparse governing information by varying procedure support and task-record sparsity. The evaluation
split uses length-4 realizations to construct the procedure knowledge base and introduces a held-out
length-7 target task at test time whose complete skill composition is absent from the stored records.
Procedure support stresses the procedure knowledge base by sweeping skill-context diversity, the
variety of contexts in which each skill is observed, over {4, 8, 16, 32} length-4 subtask orderings
populating the procedure knowledge base. For the view axis, the procedure-view interface is built
from Kitchen-defined object-factor views and a robot-state view, with the finest setting separating
them into up to eight atomic views. View resolution varies the number of available views over
{3, 4, 5, 8} by randomly merging object factors into composite views. Task-record sparsity stresses
Task Materialization by sweeping the waypoint gap H ∈{1, ..., 7}, the stride between consecutive
task-record waypoints in subtask indices, with the final goal appended whenever the stride does not
reach it. For H=4, this yields the waypoint list [0,4,7]. For each experimental configuration, we use
10 random seeds and 100 evaluation episodes per seed. We report ordered completion and per-episode
wall-clock task inference time. Figure 3 provides an overview, with details in Appendix B.4.
Baselines. We evaluate all baselines under a shared reconfiguration setting that varies procedure
support and task-record sparsity to assess how different composition mechanisms operate under
sparse governing information. Skill Retrieval (SR) first computes a multi-view score for each
candidate skill by matching each waypoint transition to the closest stored record for that skill in
each procedure view, then independently reranks skills by the averaged view-wise score. Skill
Sequence Retrieval (SSR) extends this retrieval to the combinatorial space of per-step skill
candidates, ranking candidate sequences by aggregated retrieval scores without explicitly modeling
inter-skill connectivity. Skill Chaining (SC) [46, 48] represents procedural connectivity through a
skill-level transition graph. Each skill is summarized by canonical initiation and termination states
estimated from stored endpoints, with graph edges weighted by termination-to-initiation distances.
Metrics. We report ordered completion, the fraction of environment-defined subtasks completed in
the demonstrated order [61], and task inference time, the total time spent inferring an executable skill
sequence per episode.

6.2
Procedure Knowledge Base
To answer RQ1, we evaluate RecPo and all baselines under sparse procedure specifications by varying
skill-context diversity and view resolution, with smaller values of either instantiating sparser regimes.
Figure 4 (left) shows that RecPo, which narrows candidates to view-consistent transitions, achieves
the highest ordered completion at 91.9% with only 1.0 ms of task inference per episode. SSR widens
the search to the combinatorial space of skill sequences but reaches 24.7% at 6.7 ms, suggesting that
naive scaling of the search space offers limited gains without structural constraints on composition.
Figure 4 (right) shows that task inference time increases for every method with higher skill-context
diversity or view resolution, while every RecPo configuration remains on the Pareto front of comple-
tion and time. SC declines from 56.9% to 47.5% as skill-context diversity increases and approaches
RecPo only at the highest view resolution, since the skill-level graph of SC aliases endpoints across
contexts, and higher view resolution still leaves hard negatives whose endpoints match but whose
effects do not. RecPo instead structures salience marks with skill-transition graphs, so records remain
distinguishable by the views they govern, keeping composition robust under sparse procedure support.
6.3
Task Materialization
To answer RQ2, we evaluate RecPo and all baselines under sparse task specifications by varying the
waypoint gap H ∈{1, . . . , 7} over the length-7 target task. Figure 5 shows that RecPo maintains
89.3–95.4% ordered completion across all H while its task inference time decreases from 1.8 to
0.7 ms. SR and SSR collapse as H increases, with SSR falling from 68.7% to at most 9.1% for H ≥4,
since similarity alone surfaces stored segments that do not realize demanded subtasks absent from the
procedure knowledge base. Unlike retrieval, SC and RecPo do not degrade with sparser waypoints,
since each waypoint fixes a segment to bind, and denser waypoints multiply the bindings over which
errors accumulate. SC accumulates the aliasing identified in RQ1 across these bindings and ranges
from 36.2% to 69.9%, whereas RecPo binds each segment to view-consistent transitions and keeps
the accumulated error small.

Use Cases
To answer RQ3, we test on a real Franka robot whether local updates adapt RecPo along two axes
without retraining, where view updates change what composition distinguishes and record updates
change what it can bind, in the two cases of Figure 6.
In the first case of Figure 6 (top), SC chains pouring record 104 alone and overflows the cup, reflecting
the aliasing observed in RQ1. Even with the fill-level view added, its endpoint match still weighs the
other views without salience, so stopping record 105, recorded in a different background, does not
enter the chain. RecPo weighs record 105 by its salience on the fill-level view and rematerializes the
pouring segment as a skill path chaining records 104 and 105.
In the second case of Figure 6 (bottom), SC binds record 108, a hard negative that also pours but
inverts the cup, since its endpoints match the shared background while its effect does not realize the
demanded fill-level transition, and spills the contents. RecPo binds pouring record 104 by its effect
on the fill-level view after record 107, so the background difference does not mislead the binding.
7
Conclusion
We present RecPo, which formulates the reconfiguration of skill policies as test-time composition
over an editable and inspectable procedure-memory interface. RecPo stores skill-induced transition
evidence as task-agnostic records and organizes them through procedure views. From sparse task
evidence, RecPo materializes task-specific executable skill sequences without retraining. Results
show that this structured materialization improves the success-cost trade-off of reconfiguration across
sparse procedure support and sparse task specifications, supporting adaptation through recomposition.
Limitations and future directions. Our work investigates the reconfiguration of procedural knowl-
edge through task-conditioned composition over supplied procedure views. This formulation assumes
the availability of views that expose task-relevant skill effects and their connectivity. Inadequate
view decomposition may obscure these relations and limit reconfigurability. These views can still
be separated counterfactually to match the intent of the user without retraining, and extending this
interface beyond object-centric factors and context generalization remains open. Beyond the views,
each skill record keeps only its start and end states, which suffices for composing skills whose effects
transfer across contexts but not for skills whose outcome depends on how they are executed, and
editable records that also carry such execution conditions are a natural extension.
As a future direction, the editability and inspectability of the memory interface can be leveraged for
reconfiguration across diverse scenarios, such as interactive updates on resource-limited embedded
devices and guardrails, since composition realizes only transitions supported by the memory and
editing its views and records therefore bounds what a robot can do.

A.1
Procedure Views
Procedure views provide a sparse way to govern how procedure records are read. To serve this role,
a view should change when the factor it governs changes and remain stable when other factors
vary. A view with this property exposes the local effects of skills without enumerating concrete
task bindings or full skill sequences. In the main experiments, object- or factor-centric projections
provide such views by selecting the observation dimensions of the governed factors. Other functions
of the observation can provide such views as well, such as an image embedding for each camera in
Appendix C.2.3 or the fill level of a cup in the real-world experiments of Appendix B.6.
Each view enters RecPo through its interface (αv, δv), which determines how the procedure records
are organized into skill-transition graphs and salience marks. Materialization is defined over this
organized structure for an arbitrary view set rather than for a particular choice of views. The interface
therefore separates representation from composition and serves as the basis of a modular materializa-
tion. Under this modularity, the same materialization rule applies whichever function instantiates the
views, and changes to materialization in turn need not redefine how the views are obtained.

B.1
Environment
Procedure records. We populate the procedure knowledge base with the length-4 Franka Kitchen
realizations provided by [58, 59]. Each realization starts from the common initial configuration
and executes four of seven subtasks in the relative order of the target task in Table 5. Because
the realizations instantiate different subtask subsets, the same skill may be executed after different
preceding subtasks have been completed, yielding distinct full-state contexts for the same view-local
effect. We refer to this variation in the contexts supporting reusable skill effects as skill-context
diversity.
Held-out length-7. The held-out length-7 target completes all seven subtasks, while its complete
realization is excluded from the procedure knowledge base. Figure 7 shows the common initial
configuration and the state after all seven subtasks are completed. It therefore requires skill-induced
effects observed in the length-4 records to be reused across joint contexts absent from any stored
realization. This shared relative order constrains the subtask order, leaving task materialization to
identify transition evidence for the demanded effects and compose the corresponding skills. We
further increase the waypoint gap H to remove intermediate task-state observations without changing
this ordering structure. As H grows, a single observed waypoint transition may span multiple subtask
effects, reducing the task-side evidence available for binding them to stored procedure records.
17


The held-out length-7 setting thus evaluates composition of reusable effects across unseen context
combinations under increasingly sparse task evidence.
KB construction details. A procedure realization is a sequence of 30-dimensional joint configurations.
Each configuration holds the 9 robot joints (7 arm joints and 2 gripper fingers) and the 21 object
joints. Each subtask has a goal configuration of its object joints [59], and a subtask is complete when
its object joints are within the Euclidean distance 0.3 of this goal. Each observation has the label of
the subtask that the robot executes. The label changes after the subtask is complete and its object
stops moving. We split each realization into maximal runs of the same label. Each run gives one
procedure record xi = (si, s′
i, ωi, ki) in Eq. (4). Thus a record starts where the previous record ends,
and its end observation shows the settled effect of its skill. The motion of the robot toward the next
object belongs to the next record. We use the labels only for this split. The knowledge base does not
receive a task identity or a target order.
We group the realizations by their length-4 subtask order (Table 3). The tables name each subtask by
its object as MW for the microwave, KT for the kettle, BB for the bottom burner, TB for the top burner,
LS for the light switch, SC for the slide cabinet, and HC for the hinge cabinet. 24 orders come from
the original Franka Kitchen demonstrations [58], and we add 8 orders. We build the realizations of
the added orders by stitching subtask segments of the original demonstrations. Each order gives 18 to
25 realizations. A skill-context diversity setting K contains the orders with the IDs 1 to K. Thus a
larger K only adds records to a smaller K.

B.2
Baseline Implementations
All baselines use the same stored skill records, supplied procedure views, and executable skill library.
19


Skill Retrieval (SR). SR performs transition-level retrieval and reranking. For each task-waypoint
transition, it scores each skill by matching the requested transition to the closest stored record of
that skill under each supplied procedure view. The view-wise scores are averaged to obtain a single
retrieval score for each skill, and the highest-scoring skill is selected independently for each waypoint
transition.
Skill Sequence Retrieval (SSR). SSR extends the retrieval unit from individual skill records
to stored skill subsequences. For each length-4 procedure realization, we construct retrieval items
from its contiguous skill subsequences of lengths 1, 2, and 3, preserving their observed execution
order. Each subsequence is represented under the same supplied procedure views as SR and matched
to a task-waypoint segment using the same multi-view scoring criterion. For each task segment,
SSR retrieves the highest-scoring stored subsequence. This baseline evaluates whether retrieval over
previously observed short skill subsequences is sufficient to recover the target behavior from sparse
task evidence.
Skill Chaining (SC). SC instantiates the structured procedural-representation family of skill chain-
ing and skill-graph methods [46, 48] within the shared reconfiguration interface above. It uses the
supplied procedure views when matching task waypoints to stored record endpoints, giving it access
to the same factorized procedure information as RecPo.
The distinction lies in how this evidence is organized for composition. SC summarizes the records
associated with each skill into canonical initiation and termination states in the procedure-view space,
thereby representing procedural connectivity at the skill level. Given a task query, it scores candidate
skills by agreement with the inferred waypoint changes and constructs a skill-transition graph whose
edges are weighted by termination-to-initiation distance. A bounded path search over this graph
returns the executable skill sequence. SC therefore tests whether the supplied factorized procedure
information remains sufficient after record-level evidence is consolidated into skill-level connectivity.
In contrast, RecPo retains record-derived view-local transition evidence and resolves the task-relevant
composition during materialization.

Workspace configuration. We evaluate RecPo in a cafe workspace centered on coffee preparation.
We deploy across two environments that share the goal (pour coffee) but differ in scene composition
(Figure 8), so that procedure records collected under one scene composition are reused under another.

target cup capacity. (right) Environment 2: a source whose volume matches the target cup capacity.
Robots. The workspace is equipped with a 7-DoF Franka Emika Research 3 tabletop manipulator.
Perception. The workspace is equipped with two cameras serving distinct roles. An Intel RealSense

B.6.1
Reconfiguration Scenarios
Both environments share the goal pour coffee. They differ in the source-to-target capacity relation
and in the procedure records available at deployment. The supplied views represent the source and
target factors needed for the pouring and fill-level transitions. We compare how local updates to KΩ
affect RecPo and SC under the same memory.
Case 1: interference from other views. The source contains more liquid than the target can hold.
The task therefore requires a stopping condition before the source becomes empty. The default views
expose the stored pouring transition through the source state but do not distinguish the target fill level.
We add a supplied target-fill view and a procedure record for the stopping transition. RecPo then
composes the pouring and stopping skills from their view-local effects. SC reuses the pouring skill
from endpoint compatibility alone and overfills the target.
Case 2: recovery through records. The source volume matches the target capacity. The updated
pouring segment requires one skill (Lh=1), while the full task contains two ordered skills. The
memory also contains endpoint-compatible records whose target-side effects do not match the
requested fill transition. SC can therefore select an incorrect record from endpoint compatibility. We
append the correct pouring record without changing the skill library. Its target-view transition matches
the requested effect, so RecPo binds the correct pouring skill within the two-skill task.
B.6.2
Record-Appending Scenario
For the second scenario above, procedure records are collected under three scene conditions. These
collection conditions are distinct from the two physical reconfiguration scenarios. The evaluation
combines background and manipulated-object conditions not paired during collection. We run ten
trials per method under the same lighting and nominal object placement. Sequence success requires the
ordered two-skill sequence from the eight-skill library. Task success additionally requires completing
the pour without spilling. Over the ten trials per method, RecPo achieves 10/10 sequence success
and 7/10 task success, whereas SC achieves 0/10 on both.
The three failed RecPo trials use the correct materialized sequence and are attributed to grasp,
alignment, or depth-localization errors.

C.3
Dense Task Specifications
To compare RecPo with composition from dense specifications, we evaluate adapted sub-trajectory
retrieval and graph-search mechanisms. Direct comparisons with the complete prior systems would
change the learning or control interface. STRAP [24] and graph-based imitation methods [34, 35] use
retrieved or stitched evidence for policy learning. Goal-conditioned graph planners [65, 66] instead
assume a goal-conditioned controller and learned value or temporal-distance representations. Our
setting provides a fixed library of executable skills without these learned planning components. We
therefore adapt their sub-trajectory retrieval and graph-search mechanisms to select skills from the
existing library without policy training.
To compare these mechanisms across procedure-support conditions, we average over the KB settings
listed in each condition table. In the sub-trajectory retrieval and graph-search comparisons below,
RecPo denotes the reference evaluated with the supplied full-resolution procedure views (V = 8) and
the task inputs specified below. Methods that use procedure views receive this configuration unless
stated otherwise.
C.3.1

C.3.3
Graph Search over Stored Trajectories
To assess trajectory-graph composition under sparse task specifications, we vary the waypoint
gap while retaining the same dense procedure trajectories. We adapt the offline-state graph and
shortest-path search of Test-Time Graph Search [65], a mechanism also used for subgoal selection
in Graph-Assisted Stitching [66]. Demonstration states form graph nodes, temporal edges preserve
observed order, and nearest-neighbor edges connect states across trajectories. Our adaptation uses
squared Euclidean state distances and fixed temporal-step and stitching penalties, then applies
Dijkstra’s algorithm between supplied waypoints and executes the skills along the resulting path. We
set the cross-trajectory edge penalty to 2, twice the temporal-step cost. For the task specification,
both methods receive the same sparse waypoints without interpolation at each H ∈{1, . . . , 7}. For
procedure evidence, graph search uses dense, unsegmented trajectories without procedure views,
while RecPo uses sparse procedure-record endpoints under V = 8 views. The comparison in Table 26
therefore differs in procedure evidence and composition under the same task inputs.

NeurIPS Paper Checklist
1. Claims
Question: Do the main claims made in the abstract and introduction accurately reflect the
paper’s contributions and scope?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: The abstract and introduction explicitly state the motivation and contributions.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the abstract and introduction do not include the claims
made in the paper.
• The abstract and/or introduction should clearly state the claims made, including the
contributions made in the paper and important assumptions and limitations. A [No] or
[N/A] answer to this question will not be perceived well by the reviewers.
• The claims made should match theoretical and experimental results, and reflect how
much the results can be expected to generalize to other settings.
• It is fine to include aspirational goals as motivation as long as it is clear that these goals
are not attained by the paper.
2. Limitations
Question: Does the paper discuss the limitations of the work performed by the authors?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: We outline future work that aims to address current limitations.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper has no limitation while the answer [No] means
that the paper has limitations, but those are not discussed in the paper.
• The authors are encouraged to create a separate “Limitations” section in their paper.
• The paper should point out any strong assumptions and how robust the results are to
violations of these assumptions (e.g., independence assumptions, noiseless settings,
model well-specification, asymptotic approximations only holding locally). The authors
should reflect on how these assumptions might be violated in practice and what the
implications would be.
• The authors should reflect on the scope of the claims made, e.g., if the approach was
only tested on a few datasets or with a few runs. In general, empirical results often
depend on implicit assumptions, which should be articulated.
• The authors should reflect on the factors that influence the performance of the approach.
For example, a facial recognition algorithm may perform poorly when image resolution
is low or images are taken in low lighting. Or a speech-to-text system might not be
used reliably to provide closed captions for online lectures because it fails to handle
technical jargon.
• The authors should discuss the computational efficiency of the proposed algorithms
and how they scale with dataset size.
• If applicable, the authors should discuss possible limitations of their approach to
address problems of privacy and fairness.
• While the authors might fear that complete honesty about limitations might be used by
reviewers as grounds for rejection, a worse outcome might be that reviewers discover
limitations that aren’t acknowledged in the paper. The authors should use their best
judgment and recognize that individual actions in favor of transparency play an impor-
tant role in developing norms that preserve the integrity of the community. Reviewers
will be specifically instructed to not penalize honesty concerning limitations.
3. Theory assumptions and proofs
Question: For each theoretical result, does the paper provide the full set of assumptions and
a complete (and correct) proof?
Answer: [N/A]

Justification: The paper does not include theoretical results.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include theoretical results.
• All the theorems, formulas, and proofs in the paper should be numbered and cross-
referenced.
• All assumptions should be clearly stated or referenced in the statement of any theorems.
• The proofs can either appear in the main paper or the supplemental material, but if
they appear in the supplemental material, the authors are encouraged to provide a short
proof sketch to provide intuition.
• Inversely, any informal proof provided in the core of the paper should be complemented

• Theorems and Lemmas that the proof relies upon should be properly referenced.
4. Experimental result reproducibility
Question: Does the paper fully disclose all the information needed to reproduce the main ex-
perimental results of the paper to the extent that it affects the main claims and/or conclusions
of the paper (regardless of whether the code and data are provided or not)?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: We provide a clear specification of the framework components, the experimen-
tal settings used in our evaluation, and the corresponding evaluation protocol.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include experiments.
• If the paper includes experiments, a [No] answer to this question will not be perceived
well by the reviewers: Making the paper reproducible is important, regardless of
whether the code and data are provided or not.
• If the contribution is a dataset and/or model, the authors should describe the steps taken
to make their results reproducible or verifiable.
• Depending on the contribution, reproducibility can be accomplished in various ways.
For example, if the contribution is a novel architecture, describing the architecture fully
might suffice, or if the contribution is a specific model and empirical evaluation, it may
be necessary to either make it possible for others to replicate the model with the same
dataset, or provide access to the model. In general. releasing code and data is often
one good way to accomplish this, but reproducibility can also be provided via detailed
instructions for how to replicate the results, access to a hosted model (e.g., in the case
of a large language model), releasing of a model checkpoint, or other means that are
appropriate to the research performed.
• While NeurIPS does not require releasing code, the conference does require all submis-
sions to provide some reasonable avenue for reproducibility, which may depend on the
nature of the contribution. For example
(a) If the contribution is primarily a new algorithm, the paper should make it clear how
to reproduce that algorithm.
(b) If the contribution is primarily a new model architecture, the paper should describe
the architecture clearly and fully.
(c) If the contribution is a new model (e.g., a large language model), then there should
either be a way to access this model for reproducing the results or a way to reproduce
the model (e.g., with an open-source dataset or instructions for how to construct
the dataset).
(d) We recognize that reproducibility may be tricky in some cases, in which case
authors are welcome to describe the particular way they provide for reproducibility.
In the case of closed-source models, it may be that access to the model is limited in
some way (e.g., to registered users), but it should be possible for other researchers
to have some path to reproducing or verifying the results.

7. Experiment statistical significance
Question: Does the paper report error bars suitably and correctly defined or other appropriate
information about the statistical significance of the experiments?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: We report our results with variance.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include experiments.
• The authors should answer [Yes] if the results are accompanied by error bars, confidence
intervals, or statistical significance tests, at least for the experiments that support the
main claims of the paper.
• The factors of variability that the error bars are capturing should be clearly stated (for
example, train/test split, initialization, random drawing of some parameter, or overall
run with given experimental conditions).
• The method for calculating the error bars should be explained (closed form formula,
call to a library function, bootstrap, etc.)
• The assumptions made should be given (e.g., Normally distributed errors).
36


• It should be clear whether the error bar is the standard deviation or the standard error
of the mean.
• It is OK to report 1-sigma error bars, but one should state it. The authors should
preferably report a 2-sigma error bar than state that they have a 96% CI, if the hypothesis
of Normality of errors is not verified.
• For asymmetric distributions, the authors should be careful not to show in tables or
figures symmetric error bars that would yield results that are out of range (e.g., negative
error rates).
• If error bars are reported in tables or plots, the authors should explain in the text how
they were calculated and reference the corresponding figures or tables in the text.
