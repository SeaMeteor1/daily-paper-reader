Multi-CONDOR: Learning Multistable Motion Primitives from
Demonstration
Gabriele Giuseppe Di Marzo1,4,∗,†, Mariano Ram´ırez Montero1,∗,†,
Maximilian St¨olzle1,2, Rodrigo P´erez-Dattari1,3, and Cosimo Della Santina1
Abstract— Dynamical-system-based motion policies provide
a framework for learning reactive robot motions with stability
and convergence properties. Many manipulation tasks, however,
admit several distinct goals or a continuous set of acceptable
outcomes, requiring motion primitives that preserve these alter-
natives. Accommodating both goal structures within a unified
framework with continuous, autonomous dynamics remains an
open challenge. We propose a CONDOR-based framework,
multi-CONDOR, for learning motion primitives with either
multiple discrete attractors or a continuous attracting goal
contour. The approach combines imitation learning with losses
that encourage agreement between the learned task dynamics
and latent reference systems encoding the desired attraction
structure. We provide a Lyapunov-based analysis of the conver-
gence properties of the learned motion dynamics. We evaluate
the framework on simulated benchmarks with discrete goals,
qualitative examples with continuous goal contours, and robotic
grasping experiments. In simulation, multi-CONDOR reduces
trajectory RMSE by approximately 72% compared with sepa-
rately learned single-goal motion primitives selected by a state
machine. For tasks with two and three goals, respectively, it
reduces the fraction of evaluated initial states ending outside
the goal tolerance from StableNODE’s 0.31% and 7.29% to
0% in both settings. Experiments with a Franka Emika robot
arm demonstrate successful grasping of a container by either
of its two handles using motions learned from demonstrations.
Together, these results show multi-CONDOR combines motion
reproduction with the flexibility to converge to alternative valid
outcomes.
I. INTRODUCTION
Imitation learning enables robots to acquire motion skills
from demonstrations, providing an accessible way to adapt
their behavior to new tasks. This capability is increasingly
valuable as robots enter environments where tasks vary
and frequent manual programming is impractical. General
approaches, including generative methods such as diffusion
policies [1], can represent complex behaviors, but may re-
quire substantial demonstration data [2] and do not inherently
provide stability or convergence guarantees.

Dynamical systems provide an established framework for
learning motion primitives with stability and convergence
properties. Dynamic movement primitives (DMPs) reproduce
demonstrated motions using an internal phase variable that
governs their progression [3]. If this phase advances while
physical interaction slows or blocks the robot [4], the re-
sulting mismatch can induce unnatural catch-up motions [5].
Other approaches learn autonomous vector fields that deter-
mine motion directly from the current task state, without an
independent time or phase reference [6], [7], [8], [9].
Standard point-to-point motion primitives typically encode
convergence to a single prescribed goal [3], [10]. Many
manipulation tasks instead admit multimodal goal behavior: a
robot may grasp a container at either of two handles. Other
tasks admit a continuum of acceptable outcomes, such as
grasping at any admissible point along its rim. Selecting
one endpoint in advance discards alternatives that may be
easier to reach from the current configuration or after a dis-
turbance. A disturbance can then redirect the motion toward
another valid goal, allowing the robot to complete the task
without returning to the originally selected endpoint. Motion
primitives should therefore capture both the demonstrated
approach behavior and the freedom to converge to multiple
isolated goals or a continuous goal set.

These approaches demonstrate motion generation toward
distinct goals, but do not provide a unified framework for
learning continuous, autonomous motion dynamics with both
multiple discrete attractors and continuous attracting goal
sets.
We propose a unified framework for learning motion
primitives with continuous, autonomous dynamics and either
multiple discrete attractors or a continuous attracting goal
contour. Building on CONDOR [8], we learn a task-space
vector field together with an encoding into a latent space
equipped with reference dynamics representing the desired
attraction structure. We introduce two reference systems: a
Gaussian potential for multiple isolated goals and a radially
attracting circle for a continuous goal contour. We establish
sufficient conditions for convergence to our desired set
through Lyapunov analysis, and formulate training losses that

II. METHODOLOGY
Given motion demonstrations in a task domain T ⊂RNx
and a set of admissible goals G ⊂T , we aim to learn an
autonomous dynamical system
˙x = fθ(x),
(1)
that reproduces the demonstrated motion while approaching
a goal in G. We consider both multiple isolated goals and
a continuous attracting goal contour. The velocity depends
only on the current state, with no explicit dependence on
time or a phase variable, so the eventual goal is determined
by the dynamics from the current state.
We build on CONDOR [8], which represents the task
dynamics as a neural network fθ = ϕθ ◦ψθ. The encoder
ψθ : T
→RNy maps a task state x to a latent state

task velocity. CONDOR trains the encoded task trajectories
to follow prescribed latent reference dynamics ˙y = fL(y)
with a single asymptotically stable equilibrium. We extend
this framework by introducing two reference systems that
encode either multiple discrete attractors or a continuous
attracting contour.

Remark 1 (Practical effect of unstable equilibria). Small per-
turbations along unstable directions drive trajectories away
from an unstable task-space equilibrium. In physical robot
systems, measurement noise and actuation or tracking errors
typically introduce such perturbations, making persistent
trapping at a saddle point unlikely in practice and allowing
motion to continue toward a stable goal.
This motivates three requirements on the encoder:
(R1) G ⊆E: desired goals map to latent equilibria;
(R2) E ⊆G ∪U: the convergence set contains only the
desired goals and task states mapped onto unstable
latent equilibria;
(R3) ψθ is one-to-one on G∪U: distinct goals and task states
mapped onto unstable latent equilibria have distinct
latent representations.
Under (R1) and (R2), E = G ∪U, so every task trajectory
converges to this set. Requirement (R3) keeps goal encodings
distinct and limits U to at most one task state per unstable
latent equilibrium.

contour goal encodings distinct (R3).

III. RESULTS
We first benchmark multi-CONDOR on tasks with discrete
goals, then demonstrate its application to robotic grasping
and illustrate its extension to continuous goal contours.


A. Discrete multi-attractor benchmarking
1) Datasets: We evaluate multi-CONDOR on seven 2D
motion tasks drawn manually on a digital interface: three
with two attractors and four with three attractors. The tasks
contain 6–8 and 12–24 demonstrations, respectively, with
equal numbers of demonstrations per goal. Goal positions
are estimated by clustering demonstration endpoints using k-
means, with k equal to the number of attractors, and taking
each cluster’s mean endpoint. All states are normalized to a
workspace defined by the demonstration minima and maxima
multiplied by a factor of 1.3 to provide a margin.
2) Metrics: We evaluate imitation accuracy and empirical
stability. For imitation accuracy, we simulate each system
from the demonstration initial states and compare the re-
sulting trajectories with the demonstrations using root mean
squared error (RMSE), dynamic time warping distance nor-
malized by the number of points (DTWD) [14], and Fr´echet
distance (FD) [15]. To assess stability, we simulate each
system for 5000 steps from a 16 × 16 grid covering the
workspace. We report the percentage of initial states whose
final state lies more than 5 units (approximately 1% of
the workspace width) from every goal as the “spurious
attractors” metric. We also report the mean distance from
each final state to its nearest goal (distance from goals).
3) Baselines: We compare our method with two baselines.
Single-attractor CONDORs + state machine trains one CON-
DOR model [8] per goal and selects the nearest goal’s model
at execution time, mirroring the state-space partitioning idea
of Augmented-SVM [11]. Each model combines imitation
learning with a contrastive stability loss that encourages
encoded trajectories to follow reference dynamics with a
single stable equilibrium. Each model uses the same archi-
tecture and optimizer as multi-CONDOR, specified below.
StableNODE [12], our main baseline for learning a single
autonomous dynamical system with multiple goals, uses a
neural ODE with a Lyapunov-based correction to its nominal
vector field. The correction acts when the nominal dynamics
violate a prescribed Lyapunov decrease condition. Its for-
mulation admits nonconvex Lyapunov functions, allowing
multiple attractors. We train it using the average Hausdorff
distance between generated and demonstrated trajectories,
which measures geometric similarity without requiring tem-
poral alignment. The reported results use α = ϵ = 10−2,
where α specifies the desired Lyapunov decay rate and ϵ
regularizes the correction near points where the Lyapunov
gradient vanishes.
4) Ablations: We evaluate two ablations of the discrete-
goal objective (18), retaining the multi-CONDOR network
architecture. Only imitation removes the stability loss (tra-
jectory matching and separation) and the goal-mapping loss
(λstable = λmap = 0). It tests whether fitting demonstrations
alone yields accurate imitation and reliable goal reaching
throughout the workspace. No mapping loss removes only
the goal-mapping loss (λmap = 0), retaining imitation and
stability losses. It tests whether anchoring goal encodings to
separated reference positions ξg improves imitation accuracy
and suppresses spurious attractors.
5) Multi-CONDOR implementation details: As a safe-
guard, rollout transitions that would leave T are projected
back onto its boundary. The encoder has four hidden layers
and the decoder three, all with 300 units, GELU activations
and layer normalization, and the latent space has dimen-
sion 300. The reference system is the Gaussian mixture of
Section II-C. For two attractors, the reference positions are
ξ1 = (1, . . . , 1) and ξ2 = (−1, . . . , −1); for three attractors,
they are sampled uniformly at random in the latent space.
We use horizons Hi = 10 and Hs = 7, margin m = 0.033,
weights λIL = 1, λstable = 0.4 and λmap = 0.05 with decay
factor γ = 0.99, and train for 10,000 iterations with AdamW
(learning rate 4.9×10−4, weight decay 10−5, batch size 250).
All results are averaged over five random seeds.
6) Discussion:
Table I summarizes the results. The
imitation-only ablation achieves the lowest imitation er-
rors in both settings, but its spurious-attractor rates are
approximately 17% and 37% for two and three attractors,
respectively. The full method records no spurious attractors
under our evaluation criterion, with modestly higher imita-
tion errors. Its mean distance from the goals decreases from
13.33 to 3.10 and from 28.24 to 2.69, respectively. Removing
the mapping loss also worsens both stability metrics in both
settings and substantially increases imitation errors for two
attractors.
Compared with separate single-attractor CONDOR models
selected by a state machine, multi-CONDOR achieves sub-
stantially lower imitation errors and better stability metrics
in both settings. StableNODE achieves comparable imitation
accuracy and the smallest mean distance from the goals for
two attractors. For three attractors, however, its spurious-
attractor rate is 7.29%, and its mean distance from the goals
is 17.90, compared with 2.69 for multi-CONDOR.
During StableNODE hyperparameter tuning and longer
training runs, we observed a tradeoff between imitation ac-
curacy and stability: improving one often degraded the other.
Figure 4 illustrates this qualitatively: StableNODE produces
additional spurious attractors (blue) away from the demon-
stration endpoints, with demonstrations shown in white and
generated trajectories in red. Overall, multi-CONDOR com-
bines imitation accuracy close to the imitation-only ablation
with reliable goal reaching across the evaluated initial states.
B. A multi-goal robotic grasping task
We evaluate multi-CONDOR on a robotic grasping task
in which a Franka FR3 grasps a box from either side. We
collect ten kinesthetic demonstrations, five per goal, and
learn dynamics for the 3D Cartesian end-effector position
(Figure 5).
At each control step, the learned dynamics map the current
position to a velocity, which is integrated to obtain the target
position for an impedance controller. The target orientation
is taken from the nearest recorded demonstration point, and
a separate state machine closes the gripper when the end
effector reaches either goal.

IV. CONCLUSION, LIMITATIONS, AND FUTURE WORK
Grasping a container by either handle or at admissible
points along its rim exemplifies manipulation tasks with
multiple valid endpoints. We presented a CONDOR-based
framework for learning such motions toward discrete goals
or continuous attracting goal contours. Our method reduces
mean trajectory RMSE by approximately 72% relative to
single-attractor CONDORs with a state machine. The ob-
served rate of spurious attractors falls from StableNODE’s
0.31% and 7.29% to 0% for two and three goals, respectively.
A robotic grasping demonstration further illustrates reaching
alternative goals within a single autonomous vector field.
Our Lyapunov analysis establishes sufficient conditions
for transferring convergence from the reference dynamics
to the learned task dynamics. These guarantees require
exact dynamics matching, which training only encourages. A
diffeomorphic encoder, following Euclideanizing Flows [7],
with task velocities derived through its inverse Jacobian
could enforce this condition by construction. The analysis
also requires the intended correspondence between goals
and latent attractors, including a distinct stable equilibrium
for each discrete goal. Since Gaussian interactions can shift
or merge equilibria, this extension should incorporate latent
dynamics with distinct stable equilibria fixed at the encoded
discrete goals.
The current formulation treats discrete attractors and con-
tinuous attracting contours separately. Future work could
unify these with single point attractors and limit cycles, with
convergence guarantees appropriate to each attractor type.
