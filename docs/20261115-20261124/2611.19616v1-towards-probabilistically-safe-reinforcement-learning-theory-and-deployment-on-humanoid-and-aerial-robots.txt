Abstract
Reinforcement learning (RL) enables robots to acquire complex skills, but standard RL
formulations do not guarantee that learned policies satisfy safety constraints, which limits
their use in safety-critical settings. Existing safe RL methods with provable guarantees
typically support only restricted classes of constraints and require unbiased estimators of
the objective and constraint functions, which prevents their direct integration with state-
of-the-art algorithms such as proximal policy optimization (PPO). In this paper, we extend
the recently introduced robust safe gradient flow-based reinforcement learning (RSGF-RL)
framework to overcome these limitations. Our formulation accommodates a general class
of trajectory-level safety constraints, including chance constraints such as collision avoid-
ance with a prescribed probability, and our analysis allows for biased value and gradient
estimators. We show that, with high probability, the constraint violation throughout train-
ing is bounded by the estimator bias plus a term that vanishes as the number of rollouts
grows, and decays to this level if the initial policy is unsafe. We also establish the con-
vergence properties of the iterates to approximate KKT points of the original safe RL
problem. Finally, we show that RSGF-RL implemented with PPO-style estimates enables
zero-shot sim-to-real deployment of whole-body control policies on a Unitree G1 humanoid
and collision-free waypoint navigation on a Crazyflie drone.
Keywords: Safe reinforcement learning, model-free reinforcement learning, humanoid
robots.
1. Introduction
Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a powerful tool for enabling robots to acquire
a wide range of complex skills (Hwangbo et al., 2019; OpenAI et al., 2020; Kaufmann et al.,
2023).
However, standard RL algorithms primarily optimize task performance, without
guaranteeing that the learned policies satisfy safety constraints, either during training or
after deployment. This lack of formal safety guarantees remains a major obstacle to the
adoption of RL in safety-critical applications.
Motivated by this challenge, a growing body of work on safe RL seeks to optimize
policy performance subject to prescribed safety specifications (Brunkel et al., 2022; Gar-
cia and Fernandez, 2015; Gu et al., 2024). A common approach formulates safe RL as a

constrained policy optimization problem, where the constraint requires the expected cumu-
lative value of a safety-related cost to remain below a prescribed threshold (Achiam et al.,
2017; Chow et al., 2017; Paternain et al., 2019). Several algorithms have been proposed
to solve such problems, including primal-dual methods (Paternain et al., 2023; Ding et al.,
2020), constrained policy optimization (CPO) (Achiam et al., 2017), projection-based con-
strained policy optimization (PCPO) (Yang et al., 2020), and state augmentation (Sootla
et al., 2022). Despite their empirical success, these methods generally do not guarantee
constraint satisfaction throughout the learning process, and their convergence guarantees
rely on restrictive assumptions.
An alternative approach enforces safety at the level of individual control actions, typi-
cally by combining an unconstrained RL policy with a safety filter based on control barrier
functions (CBFs) (Bejarano et al., 2025; Alshiekh et al., 2018; Cheng et al., 2019; Yang
et al., 2026; Hsu et al., 2024). While such methods can provide guarantees on the safety of
the resulting closed-loop system, they do not directly address the problem of optimizing a
policy subject to safety constraints.
In this paper, we adopt the constrained policy optimization perspective, building on
the recently introduced robust safe gradient flow-based reinforcement learning (RSGF-RL)
framework (Mestres et al., 2025b,a). RSGF-RL provides convergence guarantees to critical
points of the constrained RL problem while maintaining constraint satisfaction through-
out the learning process with high probability.
However, several limitations restrict its
integration with state-of-the-art RL algorithms and modern robotic systems:
1. Limited class of safety constraints. The existing framework considers constraints
that require that the expected value of cumulative safety costs remains below a given
threshold. However, many safety specifications arising in robotics are naturally ex-
pressed as probabilistic constraints, such as requiring that the probability of colliding
with an obstacle during an episode remains below a prescribed threshold, which are
not equivalent to the expected cumulative cost formulations.
2. Restrictive assumptions.
The theoretical guarantees of RSGF-RL as provided
in (Mestres et al., 2025b,a) require unbiased estimators of the objective and constraint
functions and their gradients. In contrast, modern RL algorithms such as Proximal
Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017), Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) (Haarnoja
et al., 2024), and DreamerV3 (Hafner et al., 2025) employ estimation procedures that
can introduce bias. This limits the integration of RSGF-RL with state-of-the-art pol-
icy optimization methods. Additionally, the conditions under which (Mestres et al.,
2025a) establishes convergence of RSGF-RL (such as the requirement that the esti-
mated RSGF-RL update law converges to the true one with probability one) are quite
restrictive.
3. Lack of hardware validation. Previous evaluations of RSGF-RL have been limited
to relatively simple simulation environments, leaving open whether its theoretical
guarantees can be combined with the computational demands and practical challenges
of RL policies for real robotic systems.

In this paper, we develop an extension of RSGF-RL that addresses these shortcomings,
and show that the resulting algorithm can be used to train RL policies for a variety of
different tasks, with zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. Our contributions are as follows.
1. First, we extend RSGF-RL to a general class of trajectory-level constraints, which in-
cludes cumulative cost and probabilistic constraints. Similarly to the Policy Gradient
Theorem (Sutton and Barto, 2018, Chapter 13), we show that gradient estimates for
this class of constraints follow from the classical likelihood-ratio method in (Glynn,
1990);
2. Second, we show that, with high probability, the constraint violation of the RSGF-RL
iterates is bounded by the estimator bias plus a sampling-error term that vanishes as
the number of rollouts N used for value and gradient estimation grows. If the initial
policy is safe, this bound holds throughout training; if it is unsafe, the violation decays
to this level. We also show that, almost surely, the RSGF-RL iterates have a limit
point that is an approximate KKT point of the constrained RL problem, with the
approximation level again given by the estimator bias plus a term that vanishes as
N →∞.
3. Third, we show that RSGF-RL implemented with (biased) PPO gradient estimates
enables zero-shot sim-to-real deployment of whole-body motor skills on a Unitree G1
humanoid robot and collision-free waypoint navigation on a Crazyflie drone. Some
assumptions of our theoretical results are not met in the practical implementation
of RSGF-RL used in these experiments. We discuss this theory-to-practice gap in
Section 4.3.

a subsequence of the RSGF-RL iterates (12) converges to a point which satisfies the KKT
conditions of (3) up to a tolerance that vanishes with εN. Hence, as εN →0 (equivalently,
as the estimator biases approach 0 and the number of rollouts N increases), such points

points are KKT points of (3) (because assumption (iii) implies MFCQ). In particular, if
estimates are constructed so that they become asymptotically unbiased, the approximation
error only depends on the number of rollouts N.

perceptron (MLP). Furthermore, the policy acts on observations rather than on the full

Next, we test RSGF-RL on crawling and squatting motion imitation tasks (Figures 2(a)-
2(b)), training a separate policy for each with motion imitation rewards (Liao et al., 2026)
and reference motions from a human motion dataset (BONES Studio, 2025). We constrain
the base height to be at least 0.4 m for squatting, and the base and head heights to be
at most 0.2 m for crawling (with a curriculum from 0.3 m to 0.2 m). Figures 1(a)-1(b)
show that, with the same g0, RSGF-RL learns the squatting and crawling motions while
respecting the height constraint, whereas vanilla PPO does not. Figure 1(a) also shows
that for the squatting motion task, RSGF-RL improves upon CPO (Achiam et al., 2017)
both in terms of safety and performance, and upon PPO-Lagrangian (Ray et al., 2019) in
safety, with very similar performance. Additionally, vanilla PPO with an added safety-aware
reward has safety and performance similar to RSGF-RL, as long as the penalty weight of
the safety-aware reward is chosen appropriately. However, the correct penalty weight is
very task-dependent, and it is unclear how to select it a priori.

4.3. Discussion on theory to practice gap
Some assumptions of our results, such as the bound (13) on the number of rollouts and the
stepsize condition 0 < ht ≤¯h, are not met in the experiments, and the probabilistic safety
guarantees in Propositions 2 and 4 are loose compared to the safety achieved in practice.
Moreover, our safety and convergence results depend on the estimator biases ζ1, ˜ζ0, ˜ζ1.
The estimates used in Section 4, detailed in Appendix H, rely on standard practical RL
techniques, such as learned value critics, multiple Adam steps on the PPO loss, and omitting
the discount weighting of the policy gradient, each of which introduces bias.
Although
techniques such as those in (D¨oring et al., 2026), (Kumar et al., 2023) can be used to bound
these biases, we do not have tight characterizations for them. Finally, the curriculum in the
crawling task makes the constraint iteration dependent, and is not directly covered by our
analysis. However, Proposition 2 still applies for policy updates with the same constraint.
5. Conclusions
We have extended RSGF-RL to a general class of constraints, including probabilistic and cu-
mulative ones, and to biased gradient and value function estimators, enabling its integration
with state-of-the-art RL algorithms such as PPO. We established safety and convergence
guarantees for the resulting algorithm, and demonstrated it on whole-body motor skills for
a G1 humanoid robot and collision-free waypoint navigation for a Crazyflie drone. Future
work will refine our assumptions and bias bounds and design estimators with good practi-
cal performance and provable guarantees, in order to narrow the gap between theory and
practice discussed in Section 4.3.

Appendix H. Training details
Algorithm 1 proposes a practical implementation of RSGF-RL as used in Section 4. It
summarizes how the estimates bV1, d
∇V 1 and d
∇V 0 entering (11) are computed at each
iteration.
At iteration t, the policy πθt is rolled out in N parallel environments for Tr
steps each (cf. Table 2); environments that terminate are reset and keep running, so each
environment produces a segment containing one or more, possibly partial, episodes. The
constraint estimates are built from the discounted cost-to-go of R1 along these segments,
using a learned cost critic Wψ as the baseline b1,t in (7).
For the objective, instead of
forming a gradient explicitly, we run a standard PPO-clip update starting from θt and
define d
∇V 0(θt) as the resulting parameter displacement divided by ht, so that −htd
∇V 0(θt)
is exactly the PPO step. Although this quantity is not an unbiased estimate of ∇V0(θt) (as
it accumulates J Adam-preconditioned minibatch steps evaluated along the PPO iterates),
it is uniformly bounded, and hence satisfies (9)-(10) for some ˜ζ0, and we have found works
well in practice. In this way, problem (11) acts as a filter on the PPO step: if ξ = −d
∇V 0(θt)
is feasible for (11), then ˆR(θt) = −d
∇V 0(θt) and the update coincides with the PPO iterate;
otherwise, the PPO step is replaced by ht ˆR(θt).
In Algorithm 1, Lclip is as defined in (Schulman et al., 2017, Equation 9) and KL is the
KL divergence averaged over the rollout states. Adam uses gradient-norm clipping, and
its moment estimates are carried over across iterations. The cost critic Wψ has the same
architecture as the value critic and receives the same observations.
Regarding the baseline comparison in Figure 1(a), we point out that PPO-Lagrangian
follows the implementation in OpenAI (2019) (Lagrange multiplier λ = softplus(ϑ) initial-
ized to 1 and updated with Adam, learning rate 0.05), with all PPO settings matching the
PPO baselines in Table 2 and the cost limit applied to the mean per-step cost. On the
other hand, CPO (Achiam et al., 2017) uses the parameters in Table 3.
