Abstract
As real-robot data collection scales, collection-system design shapes both operator efficiency and the behaviors
learned by robot policies. Workspace limitations, control-interface friction, and latency can introduce pauses,
detours, and corrective motions into otherwise successful demonstrations. We investigate these effects across
embodiment, teleoperation control, and data consistency, combining a kinematic study of 25 dual-arm robots,
arXiv:2611.22388v1  [cs.RO]  17 Nov 2026


controlled teleoperation experiments, and real-robot evaluation of fine-tuned π0.5 policies. The embodiment study
shows that positional reachability overstates orientation-aware task coverage and that modest shoulder motion can
substantially expand the effective workspace. Teleoperation experiments show that parallelizing body-part control
reduces task completion time by 43–53%, while compliant control improves success and reduces peak contact
forces on constrained and contact-rich tasks. Policy-learning experiments show that demonstrations must capture
the visual information underlying action choices: active-search demonstrations improve arm-selection accuracy
from chance (50%) to 100%. Calibration experiments reveal that mixed-calibration training data remain usable in
our tested configurations when an inference-aligned subset is retained, whereas inference-time miscalibration
causes all 30 trials to fail. Policies also retain approximately 90% of the extra path length in detour-contaminated
demonstrations, showing how inefficient operator motions persist in learned behavior. Together, these findings
connect collection-system design to demonstration quality and downstream policy execution. They motivate
evaluating effective workspace, control efficiency, observation completeness, calibration alignment, and motion
quality before scaling collection, and addressing system-induced compensations at their source.

Introduction
As real-robot data collection continues to scale, the design of the collection system becomes increasingly consequential
for both collection efficiency and demonstration quality. The robot’s embodiment determines which task poses are
feasible, the teleoperation interface shapes how operators execute motions, and the sensing and calibration setup
determines how those motions are represented in the recorded data. These system choices influence both the effort
required to collect demonstrations and the behaviors that robot policies can learn from them. Understanding their effects
is therefore an essential part of building a reliable data collection pipeline.
Consider an ordinary grasping task. The robot may respond quickly to commands, yet the video presented to the
operator can lag behind its actual motion. The operator notices an overshoot only after it has occurred, pauses to wait
for feedback, and then corrects the trajectory. Limited workspace or frequent control-mode switching can introduce
further adjustments. The grasp may ultimately succeed, while the recorded demonstration includes the pauses, detours,
and corrections needed to compensate for the collection system. Task success alone does not reveal the quality of the
resulting demonstration. Some compensatory motions may subsequently be reproduced by a policy trained on those
trajectories.
Other data issues arise from the information and coordinate relationships available during collection. An operator may
choose an action using visual cues outside the robot’s recorded camera views, leaving the policy without the evidence
needed to make the same decision. Differences in joint-zero calibration can cause the same recorded joint values
to correspond to different physical configurations across robots or collection sessions. These issues affect different
aspects of the learning problem: whether observations support action choices, whether recorded states and actions are
consistent, and whether demonstrated motions are efficient. Demonstration quality therefore requires examining the
relationship between the collection setup, the recorded signals, and the resulting policy behavior.
We investigate these issues through three complementary perspectives (Figure 1):
• Embodiment (§2): Can the robot perform the task in the required workspace? Using kinematic models of 25
dual-arm robots, we examine how arm morphology, waist motion, and end-effector length affect orientation-
aware workspace coverage and joint-limit margin.
• Teleoperation control (§3): Can the operator execute the task smoothly and efficiently? Through representative
tasks and controlled experiments, we study control parallelism, motion mapping, compliant control, system
latency, and peripheral configuration, examining their effects on task completion, operator efficiency, and
interaction quality.
• Data quality (§4): Are demonstrations informative, consistent, and efficient? Through fine-tuning and real-
robot evaluation of π0.5 policies (Physical Intelligence et al., 2025), we examine how missing visual decision
cues, joint-zero calibration mismatch, and redundant demonstrated motions affect learned behavior.
These studies provide empirical evidence under representative tasks and configurations, together with practical rec-
ommendations for embodiment selection, teleoperation design, and data acceptance. Our central perspective is that
collection-system design and demonstration quality should be evaluated together: system limitations can shape

those trajectories teach. For practitioners scaling real-robot data collection, this motivates checking task feasibility,

Key Takeaways
Embodiment: can the robot do it? (§2)
• Select embodiments by orientation-aware effective workspace, not positional reach. Reach overstates
suitability: humanoid and wheeled-lift arms reach 55% and 53% of a sagittal slice through the shoulder but
cover only 41% and 33% of tool orientations, against 91% and 75% for table-mounted arms.
• For tabletop work, non-humanoid layouts have the advantage. At their best table heights, table-mounted
arms reach 80% downward coverage and wheeled-lift arms 68% forward coverage, against 52%/58% for the
humanoid.
• Treat a small amount of waist motion as the cheapest workspace multiplier, and prioritize pitch and yaw
over roll. Allowing 15 cm of shoulder translation enlarges the reachable cross-section 3.4–5.5×; in human
motion data, pitch and yaw move 1.2–1.9× as much as roll.
• Do not buy reach with tool length. A 10 cm longer tool adds 39% reachable positions but pushes solutions
toward joint limits; weigh coverage and joint margin equally, and extend the workspace through the waist first.
Control system: can the operator control it smoothly? (§3)
• Parallelize control and eliminate high-frequency mode switching. Moving the waist to IK and the base and
head to the headset cut completion time by 43–53%; train operators to overlap motions (2–4.5× throughput
gains over 5–7 weeks).
• Choose the motion mapping by task range and continuity. Prefer absolute mapping for large-range tasks that
require continuous trajectories; prefer relative mapping with clutching for limited ranges or frequent operator
repositioning.
• Provide compliance for mechanism-constrained, continuous-contact, and bimanual closed-chain tasks.
Force control reached 100% success against 80–87% for position control and cut peak contact force by 55–71%;
such tasks make up 18.5% of 207k collection hours, and a further 38.7% would benefit. Stiff position control
remains adequate for low-contact pick-and-place.
• Optimize the measured human-in-the-loop latency, not algorithm runtime. Sub-millisecond filters can add
over 200 ms of phase lag; the slowest required body part (typically gripper, waist, or base) sets the loop. Aim
for latency below about 100 ms; further reductions yield diminishing returns.
• Match peripherals to the task and to shift length. Use screens for long fixed-station sessions (about 62 min
before discomfort versus 19 min in VR); use VR with body tracking for active viewpoint control and whole-body
tasks; use a SpaceMouse or force-feedback exoskeleton for fine or contact-sensitive work.
Data quality: can the data explain the action? (§4)
• Make sure the recorded observations explain every decision. For occlusions, out-of-view targets, and
branching decisions, collect beyond line of sight (BLOS) and make active observation part of the demonstration:
arm-choice accuracy rose from chance (50%) to 100%.
• Make collection–inference calibration alignment a pre-deployment gate. Record calibration versions, verify
joint readings at a reference pose, and compensate per-joint zero offsets; a miscalibrated inference robot failed
in all 30 trials. Mixed-calibration history need not be discarded, but keep an inference-aligned anchor subset
(66.7% vs. 26.7% success with vs. without it); zero-mean offsets do not cancel out (20%).
• Keep actions clean, and accept demonstrations on path efficiency, continuity, and duration, not success
alone. Policies retain about 90% of the extra path length but only 45% of the extra jerk; 80% detour data slowed
execution by 29%. Fix system-induced corrections at the source, and keep purposeful information-gathering
motions.
Overall
Before scaling collection, validate the embodiment’s effective workspace, operator efficiency, and data
learnability on representative tasks.

Embodiment Design: Can the Robot Carry Out the Operator’s Intent?
Embodiment design should first match the manipulation requirements of the target tasks. That an arm can reach a target
position does not mean it can operate there with the orientation the task requires, or that it retains sufficient joint range
after arriving. Even when a target pose is feasible, if the required configuration lies close to joint limits, or the body
posture must be adjusted frequently to cover the work area, the operator’s burden increases and extra adjustment motions
enter the demonstration. Reachability is strongly constrained by the end-effector orientation the task requires, so
the effective workspace is smaller than the reachable workspace. Degrees of freedom and morphology should
therefore be chosen according to the workspace in which the task is best performed.
2.1
Morphology Should Match the Task’s Operating Directions
Different tasks impose different requirements on manipulation position and end-effector orientation. In tabletop grasping,
placing, and wiping, for example, the end effector typically approaches the target from the front or from above. Using
URDF models of 25 mainstream humanoid and non-humanoid dual-arm robots, we test multiple tool-axis directions
at each of 10,075 positions in a sampled volume and use inverse kinematics (IK) to compute the fraction of feasible
directions at each position.
Positional reachability does not imply that the required end-effector orientations are feasible, and so cannot
by itself guide the choice of morphology. The top row of Figure 2 shows reachability and orientation coverage for
the right arm of three morphologies within a 0.9 m × 1.8 m × 1.8 m volume, with 64 discretized tool-axis directions
tested at each position. Reachability only requires that at least one direction is solvable, whereas coverage reflects how
many tool-axis directions each position can satisfy. In the sagittal slice through the right shoulder, in the best case the
humanoid and the wheeled dual-arm with lift have similar reachability (55% and 53%) and mean coverage (41%
and 33%), while the table-mounted dual-arm is markedly higher on both (91% and 75%). Comparing arms by
positional reach alone easily overestimates their suitability for a specific manipulation task.
Once the end-effector orientations required by tabletop tasks are taken into account, the effective workspaces of the
different morphologies diverge (Figure 2, middle and bottom rows). Centered on gravity and on the robot’s forward
direction (+X), we select 9 downward and 11 forward directions within a 45◦cone and maximize the mean coverage
of each set over table height. At their respective optimal table heights, the table-mounted dual-arm attains the highest
downward coverage (80%) with 55% forward coverage; the wheeled dual-arm with lift attains the highest forward
coverage (68%) with 63% downward; and the humanoid attains 52% and 58%, respectively. For tabletop manipulation,
non-humanoid morphologies hold an advantage over humanoids.
2.2
Limited Waist Motion Substantially Enlarges the Tabletop Workspace
Can the workspace limitation of humanoid morphologies on tabletop tasks be compensated for by waist motion? Waist
motion changes the position and orientation of the shoulder, thereby extending the arm’s effective workspace. To
quantify this effect, we construct representative humanoid and non-humanoid arms by averaging the link parameters of
each class and compare their workspaces when the shoulder is allowed to translate.
Figure 3 shows reachable cross-sections at four end-effector orientations (0◦, −30◦, −60◦, −90◦). Allowing the
shoulder to translate within a sphere of radius 15 cm enlarges the reachable cross-sectional area to 3.4–5.5× that
of a fixed shoulder, substantially relieving the humanoid’s tabletop limitation. In a side-by-side comparison, the
humanoid’s reachable cross-section with shoulder translation can be up to 3× that of the non-humanoid.
The individual waist degrees of freedom also contribute differently. We analyze 5,496 human motion sequences from the
AMASS dataset (Mahmood et al., 2019) together with 15 hours of whole-body mobile tabletop manipulation captured
with PICO trackers. In both datasets, the geometric means of the pitch/roll and yaw/roll ratios of torso-relative-to-pelvis
rotation exceed 1 (AMASS: 1.61 and 1.88; PICO: 1.52 and 1.21; Table 1), indicating that pitch and yaw carry larger
relative motion. This agrees with their kinematic roles—pitch repositions the shoulder and yaw changes the arm’s
approach direction—whereas the function of roll can largely be substituted by base or leg motion. System design
should therefore evaluate the waist’s ability to dynamically reposition the shoulder, rather than relying solely
on changing arm morphology or link lengths. Where waist DoFs must be limited, pitch and yaw should be
prioritized.

2.3
End-Effector Length Trades Coverage Against Local Joint Margin
End-effector length changes the geometric relation between the wrist and the actual point of operation, and hence the
joint configuration required for the same task pose. A longer end effector can extend positional coverage but may also
push the joints closer to their limits, so its length must balance reach against joint margin.

and joint limits for each class are derived from 25 mainstream robot URDFs, and planar reachable cross-sections are

We increase the wrist-to-TCP distance from about 14.31 cm to 24.31 cm and compare the two configurations over the
same 10,075 sampled positions. The number of positions with at least one solvable direction rises from 2,761 to 3,851,
an increase of about 39.5%. However, over the target directions solvable in both configurations, the IK solutions of the
longer tool exhibit higher joint-limit cost (Figure 4). This not only reduces dexterity at the target (Yoshikawa, 1985) but
also raises the risk of approaching kinematic singularities and weakens dynamic stability during operation.
When choosing tool length, task coverage and joint margin should be optimized jointly and weighted equally. For
tasks that require frequent fine adjustment, avoid unnecessary tool extension once grasp size and approach requirements
are met, so as to preserve joint margin.
Overall, embodiment design should coordinate arm selection, waist DoFs, and end-effector length according to the
positions and orientations the task requires. When the morphology struggles to meet task requirements, extend the
effective workspace through the waist first, rather than trading tool length for reach. Sound embodiment design
determines not only whether a task can be completed, but also how many extra adjustments the operator must make.
Whether operator intent can be mapped effectively onto this effective workspace—making full use of the embodiment’s
motion capability—is the responsibility of the control system.
3
Control System: Can the Operator Convey Intent to the Robot Smoothly?
The control system is the bridge between human intent and robot motion. An ideal control system should map the
operator’s motion intent accurately into executable target trajectories that fully exploit the embodiment, while
minimizing the extra cognitive and physical load the operator incurs in adapting to the interface. Frequent
mode switching, unintuitive motion mapping, repeated corrections caused by contact errors, and latency and peripheral
limitations can all lengthen demonstrations and introduce unnecessary pauses and adjustments into trajectories. We
evaluate control systems along three axes: operating efficiency, ease of learning, and task success.
3.1
Control Parallelism: Reducing Serial Operation and Mode Switching
Whole-body control (WBC) coordinates the mobile base, waist, head, both arms, and end effectors simultaneously.
Operating all joints and subsystems in parallel significantly improves efficiency. Parallelism alone, however, is not
sufficient; an intuitive human–robot kinematic mapping is equally important. Conversely, if multiple subsystems share
one controller and rely on mode switching, the operator must perform base motion, waist pitch, viewpoint adjustment,
and arm reaching serially. This lengthens operation and readily introduces discontinuous pauses into demonstrations,
reducing data quality.

3.2
Motion Mapping: Balancing Continuity and Flexibility
Motion mapping determines how the operator’s movements are converted into robot motion targets; its intuitiveness
affects efficiency, learning cost, and operator preference. Absolute mapping maps the operator’s current hand or
body pose to a robot target pose through a coordinate transform and scaling. Under fixed calibration and mapping
parameters, the same human pose always corresponds to the same robot target, so spatial relationships are intuitive and
no re-referencing is needed, which favors motion continuity. However, its operating range is bounded by the human
workspace and the mapping scale, and large-range operation usually requires coordinated waist or base motion.
Relative mapping converts increments of controller motion into increments of the robot target pose. Through clutching,
the operator can reposition themselves and keep controlling the robot within a limited human workspace. This suits
scenarios that require frequent changes of operator posture, but clutching and control-state switching introduce extra
pauses that break trajectory continuity (Figure 7).
In large-scale collection, the choice of mapping is also shaped by operator habits, wearable burden, and sustained-
operation needs. Line-of-sight (LOS) collection, for example, usually lets operators observe the scene directly and
reposition freely, so relative mapping is highly practical for long continuous shifts. Absolute mapping maintains a stable
spatial correspondence, but its convenience depends on the body-tracking range, control devices, and environment.

3.3
Compliant Control: Reducing Corrections Caused by Contact Errors
Contact tasks require the robot to accommodate both the target trajectory and environmental constraints. When opening
a door, the end effector is constrained by the hinge trajectory; when wiping, contact must be maintained continuously;
and when carrying a rigid object with two hands, the two end effectors form a closed kinematic chain. In these tasks,
high-stiffness position control tries to track the commanded target strictly and may generate large contact or internal
forces when the target is inconsistent with the true constraint, forcing the operator to adjust the target repeatedly.
Mechanism-constrained, contact-rich, and bimanual closed-chain tasks place higher demands on compliance;
their efficiency and success rate are strongly affected by it.
Compliant control lets the robot adjust its motion response to contact, reducing reliance on precise trajectory tracking.
Common approaches include impedance control, which regulates the force response to motion deviation through
equivalent stiffness and damping (Hogan, 1985), and admittance control, which adjusts the motion target according to
measured external force, allowing the robot to accommodate environmental constraints within a certain range.
The need for compliant control in real collection is widespread. Our records contain about 20,253 task entries totaling
207,030 collection hours. By collection time, 18.5% of the data clearly involves mechanism-constrained, continuous-
contact, or bimanual closed-chain tasks that require compliant control, and a further 38.7% corresponds to tasks such
as smoothing, pressing, folding, tensioning, and tight fits that would benefit from it (Table 4). To verify the role of
compliant control in practice, we select four tasks—block stacking, door opening, whiteboard wiping, and bimanual box
carrying—each performed by an experienced teleoperator under force and position control, 30 trials per configuration.
Force control achieved 100% success on all four tasks; position control also reached 100% on block stacking, but only
83.3%, 80%, and 86.7% on door opening, whiteboard wiping, and box carrying, respectively (Table 3). Compared with
low-contact pick-and-place, compliant control shows a clearer success-rate advantage in mechanism-constrained,
continuous-contact, and bimanual closed-chain tasks.

3.4
System Latency: Optimizing the Full Loop and Motion Response
Teleoperation depends heavily on the closed loop “operator issues a command, robot executes, visual feedback returns,
operator adjusts” (Sheridan and Ferrell, 1963). System latency arises not only from command transmission and
computation, but also from signal filtering, robot dynamic response, and the generation and transmission of visual
feedback. Accumulated latency in the control chain leads operators to misjudge the robot’s state and make unnecessary
overcompensating motions. In a simplified model where the required body parts respond in parallel (Figure 9), the total
feedback latency is
Ttotal = Tctrl + Tproc + max

where R is the set of body parts required by the task, Tctrl is the command transmission delay, Tproc is the equivalent
delay of control processing, Ti is the motion-response delay of part i, and Tvideo is the visual feedback delay. The
model assumes all parts respond simultaneously, so overall completion is determined by the slowest necessary
part. Robot design should therefore consider whole-body tracking performance: substantially speeding up a single
part yields limited overall improvement. In our experience, the end effector, waist, and base are the most common
efficiency bottlenecks.
In practice, computation time and motion-response delay differ substantially. We observe that pose filtering, motion
retargeting, and joint filtering can each take under 1 ms per call while the corresponding motion response still lags by
tens of milliseconds; some filter configurations take less than 0.01 ms yet introduce over 200 ms of phase lag at particular
motion frequencies. Latency should therefore not be evaluated by algorithm execution time, but measured directly
as the time between command input and actual motion response.
The effect of latency is especially pronounced in fine tasks. In simulated teleoperation, we set target sizes of 2 cm, 4 cm,
and 8 cm, inject varying amounts of extra latency into the control chain, and measure the time operators need to reach
the target pose. For the 2 cm target, increasing extra latency from 0 to 200 ms raises mean completion time from about

human-in-the-loop response, prioritizing the dominant latency sources—filtering, robot motion response, and
visual feedback—rather than merely pursuing lower algorithmic computation time.

3.5
Control Peripherals: Balancing Capability and Sustained Workload
Control peripherals determine how the operator perceives the task environment and conveys intent to the robot. Devices
must provide sufficient spatial information and motion-control capability while accounting for wearable burden, fatigue,
and efficiency over long collection sessions. Display and motion-input devices should therefore be chosen according to
the task and designed together as one interaction system. Display devices shape spatial perception and sustained
working capacity. 2D and 3D screens require no headset and suit long sessions at a fixed station. VR headsets let
the viewpoint follow head motion, providing more direct spatial perception and viewpoint control, and suit tasks that
require moving, bending, and actively searching for targets; prolonged wear, however, can cause fatigue or motion
sickness (LaViola, 2000). In our device tests, mean session durations before discomfort were 62.11, 63.14, and 18.71
minutes for 2D screens, 3D screens, and VR headsets, respectively (Table 5).

Motion-input devices must match the task’s control DoFs, precision, and range. Exoskeletons establish a fairly
direct mapping between human motion and robot joints or end effectors and, with force feedback, can convey contact
information to the operator; their weight, mechanical constraints, and calibration complexity, however, add burden. A
SpaceMouse suits small-range six-DoF fine adjustment, but continuous large-range operation typically needs additional
control mechanisms. Hand controllers offer a compromise between portability and range: different control modes
support fine adjustment, and combining them with headsets, body tracking, and automatic control modules supports
more complex whole-body operation.
Peripheral selection should jointly consider the task’s spatial-perception and motion-control needs and the
operator’s sustained working capacity. For fixed stations and long sessions, screens and lightweight input devices are
preferable; for tasks that require active viewpoint adjustment and whole-body coordination, VR and body tracking are
appropriate; for fine manipulation or tasks that need contact feedback, a SpaceMouse or a force-feedback exoskeleton
can be introduced as needed. Peripheral configuration should be driven by task fit and long-term efficiency, not by a
pursuit of greater immersion or more input DoFs.
4
Data Quality: No Missing Information, No Redundancy, Full Consistency
Data quality sets the ceiling of imitation learning; at its core are complete information, motion without redundancy,
and end-to-end consistency.
Complete information. If the operator relies on cues that the data do not record, the model cannot learn the causal basis
of the action. For instance, if the operator closes the gripper based on a side view while the data record only an occluded
front view, the model sees the gripper close “for no reason.” Such a gap in the sensing configuration directly undermines
learnability; observations must contain all the information needed for the decision.
No redundant motion. Ineffective micro-adjustments and exploratory wobbles caused by collection latency are labeled
as positive examples. Fitting this behavioral noise wastes model capacity and causes hesitation and oscillation at
deployment.
End-to-end consistency. Inconsistency stems mainly from two sources. The first is error internal to the data, such as
timestamp misalignment that offsets state and action by several frames, or inaccurate hardware calibration, which the
model learns as if it were real structure. The second is cross-domain error at inference, such as different joint zero
positions between the deployment and collection robots, or changes in inference or motion-control parameters; such
heterogeneous errors are out-of-distribution perturbations the model has never seen and readily cause compounding
errors and task failure.

4.1
Missing Information: Visual State Must Explain the Action
A demonstration records what the robot did, but not necessarily why the operator did it. When an action depends on a
visual cue, the model must have access to that information before the action occurs. The absence of such a signal cannot
be compensated by more data.
To test this, we design a simple task: a block is placed outside the robot’s initial field of view, and the robot must pick
and place it with the nearer hand. We compare three demonstration protocols (Figure 12). In LOS-known, the operator
stands in the scene and can see the block directly, and so reaches with the nearer hand without looking; the robot’s initial
observation lacks this cue, leaving the action without a causal basis. In BLOS-known, the operator’s view is aligned with
the robot’s, but the operator still reaches directly based on prior knowledge of the block position. In BLOS-search, the
operator first has the robot lean over to look, confirms the block’s location, and only then chooses which hand to use.
The results differ sharply (Table 6). In arm-selection accuracy, BLOS-known (53%) and LOS-known (about 50%),
both lacking the initial visual cue, are close to random guessing, whereas BLOS-search, which includes active search,
reaches 100%. Its 47-point improvement clearly exceeds trial-to-trial variation (30 trials per condition; 95% confidence
interval of the difference 27–64 points). In subsequent pick-and-place success, BLOS-search again performs best,
rising from 40% for BLOS-known to 60%, while LOS-known achieves only 33%. For tasks with occlusion or blind
spots, demonstrations must capture the complete “observe, decide, act” sequence, so that information-gathering
motions such as leaning, turning the head, and adjusting the viewpoint enter the training data, and the image
history needed for the decision is retained. This is not a mere performance optimization but decides task success:
when key decision information is absent, the model can only guess from scene priors, whereas once demonstrations
include active observation, an otherwise ambiguous action choice acquires a clear visual basis.

4.2
Inconsistency: Calibration and Collection–Inference Alignment
The model takes vision and proprioceptive state as input and predicts the next action. If these are systematically
misaligned in the data, the model may learn the misalignment as if it were a regularity. Timestamp offsets make state
and action fail to correspond; joint zero-offset miscalibration pairs the same image with different proprioceptive states.
Both can degrade model performance.
Categorizing our robot data, we find the relevant factors to be joint zero positions, kinematic parameters, torque sensors,
dynamics, and camera intrinsics and extrinsics, with overall machine quality finally judged by a whole-robot consistency
check (Figure 13). Their effects differ. For VLA and world-action-model (WAM) training data, the joint zero position
most directly affects the state representation, while the other calibrations act mainly through pose computation, control
tracking, or downstream data processing. We therefore focus on joint zero positions and analyze how zero offsets affect
model inference.
Prior work has shown that predicting delta actions outperforms predicting absolute actions (Feng et al., 2026), but
it has not examined whether, under a delta-action representation, joint zero offsets degrade model performance (see

To emulate different zero-offset scenarios, we start from complete demonstrations and inject fixed offsets batch-wise into
the 14 joint states. Two variables—whether clean data is included and whether offsets cancel out—define four training
mixtures (Figure 14): A, all clean data; A′, 25 clean episodes plus 75 episodes with same-sign (biased) offsets; B, no
clean data, with zero-mean offsets of opposite signs; and B′, no clean data, with biased offsets. The test task has the left
arm take a test tube from an orange rack and hand it to the right arm, which inserts it into a dark-blue rack. The early
grasping phase is relatively coarse, while the later handover and insertion require more precise bimanual coordination.
A′, which keeps only 25% clean data, reaches 66.7% success in the precise phase, essentially on par with the all-clean A
at 63.3%. With 30 trials per group, the 95% confidence half-width of each success rate is about 14–16 points; in this
experiment, a large share of zero-offset data did not damage performance.
The real gap is between A′ and B′. The two share 75% of their data and differ only in replacing the remaining 25%
clean data with another offset group, yet success drops from 66.7% to 26.7%, a 40-point difference (95% CI of the
difference about 15–59 points) that exceeds trial variation. This suggests that a small amount of data aligned with the
inference zero position may serve as a crucial reference. Configuration B provides another counterexample: even though
positive and negative offsets cancel out overall, success is only 20%. A zero-mean offset does not guarantee a consistent
state–action relationship within each episode. Hence, historical data need not be discarded wholesale because of
differing zero-calibration batches, but the dataset should contain baseline trajectories aligned with the inference
robot.
Why did part of the offset data not hurt performance? We attribute this mainly to a change in how the model weighs vision
against proprioception (Figure 15): it focuses more on visual feedback than on proprioceptive values. In heterogeneous
data with joint-zero offsets, the same joint reading may correspond to entirely different physical poses and actions

4.3
Redundancy: Meaningless Human-Induced Motion in Actions
The embodiment limits, control mappings, and latency discussed above often force operators to pause, detour, or correct
repeatedly. These compensatory motions help complete the current task, yet they are written into the training data as
positive examples. How much of this redundant motion does the model learn, and in what form does it appear? Our
experiments suggest that motion trajectories may expose data contamination earlier than final task success does.
We use a bimanual block-transfer task: the right arm picks and places a block, then the left arm repeats the operation.
Four models are each trained on 100 demonstrations, of which 0%, 20%, 50%, or 80% are replaced by trajectories
containing pauses and detours. Compared with clean demonstrations, these trajectories have on average 33% longer
wrist paths, about 1.8× the joint jerk, and 1.6 rather than 1.1 single-arm pauses longer than 0.3 s (Figure 16).
Real-robot inference reveals an interesting asymmetry: the model smooths some of the motion roughness but retains
most of the redundant path (Table 7). From 0% to 80% mixture, the demonstrations’ mean wrist path grows by
0.215 m and the policy’s by 0.194 m, i.e., the policy retains about 90% of the added distance; by contrast, only about
45% of the increase in joint jerk is retained. The model partially smooths abrupt accelerations but still tends to follow
the detours in the demonstrations. Data cleaning should therefore target redundant corrections in the trajectory itself;
improving smoothness alone still leaves much ineffective motion. Pauses and corrections can also take different forms
at inference. Low-speed events longer than 0.3 s do not vary consistently with the mixture ratio, but speed peaks per
meter of path rise from 10.8 to 19.1 and direction reversals from 3.2 to 10.0. Even at 20% mixture, where mean joint
jerk is still close to the clean model, the velocity mismatch at boundaries between consecutive action chunks already
rises from 0.36 to 0.58, reaching 0.78 at 80% (Table 7). Some motion defects first appear as repeated speed changes and
planning discontinuities, and only later as growth in overall jerk.
One explanation worth further verification lies in the policy’s replanning mechanism (Black et al., 2024; Zhao et al.,
2023): the model periodically generates new action chunks, and after being influenced by pause and correction
trajectories, consecutive chunks may differ more in direction and speed. We conjecture that in the training set, pauses
and disturbances make the same state co-occur with more uncertain actions, amplifying velocity differences between
adjacent outputs.

Acceptance of demonstration data should therefore consider path efficiency, motion continuity, and task duration

operating cost in the collection system may ultimately turn into a cost the robot keeps paying during autonomous

Related Work
Data sources for manipulation learning.
Learning from demonstration (Argall et al., 2009) underpins diffusion-based
and generalist VLA policies (Black et al., 2024; Chi et al., 2023; Physical Intelligence et al., 2025; Ze et al., 2025).
Their data supply spans real-robot corpora (AgiBot-World-Contributors et al., 2025; Khazatsky et al., 2024; Open
X-Embodiment Collaboration, 2024; Wang et al., 2026a), egocentric datasets (Grauman et al., 2021; Hoque et al., 2025;
Punamiya et al., 2026) and human-to-robot learning (Kareer et al., 2024; Qiu et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025; Zhou et al.,
2026), portable UMI-style collection (Chi et al., 2024; Han et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2025; Zhaxizhuoma et al., 2024), and
simulation-based demonstration generation (Mandlekar et al., 2023; Mu et al., 2025). Together, these directions broaden
scale and diversity, while leaving a common requirement: observations and actions must provide consistent, learnable
supervision.
Teleoperation and collection methods.
Low-cost bimanual and whole-body systems (Fu et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2023),
immersive visual and haptic interfaces (Cheng et al., 2024; Ding et al., 2025), and coordinated mobile manipulation
systems (Lu et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026) expand collection capabilities. Motion reconstruction and retargeting
connect human movement to robot execution (Araújo et al., 2026; Xiao et al., 2026). Autonomous acquisition and
trajectory generation (Li et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025), deliberate perturbations (Huang et al., 2025), and human
corrective guidance (Wang et al., 2026b) further improve collection efficiency or informativeness. We complement these
approaches by measuring how interface parallelism, mapping, latency, and peripheral burden affect operator throughput
and demonstration continuity.
Embodiment, calibration, and action representations.
Embodiment co-design (Ha et al., 2026) and robot-world and
hand-eye calibration (Wise et al., 2026) address physical capability and coordinate alignment, while kinematics-aware
policies and action-space studies examine joint–Cartesian and spatial–temporal representations (Feng et al., 2026;
19


Ganapathi et al., 2022). Our analysis connects orientation-aware workspace and joint margin to collection demands, and
examines delta joint actions under heterogeneous calibration across collection and deployment robots.
Demonstration quality and system effects.
Prior work relates imitation-learning performance to demonstration quality,
operator proficiency, action divergence, and state diversity (Belkhale et al., 2023; Mandlekar et al., 2021). Observation
mismatch and causal confusion (de Haan et al., 2019; Swamy et al., 2022) explain why successful demonstrations
can remain difficult to learn from; interactive correction (Ross et al., 2011) offers one remedy. Established studies of
impedance control (Hogan, 1985), teleoperation delay (Sheridan and Ferrell, 1963), and cybersickness (LaViola, 2000)
provide foundations for analyzing collection-system constraints. We connect these concerns by tracing how embodiment
and control choices increase operator effort and introduce missing information, calibration inconsistency, or redundant
motion into policy training.
6
Conclusion
From embodiment design through the control system to data quality, our analysis points to a single chain: the design of
the collection system determines how much extra effort operators must spend, and that effort can in turn shape
the training data and the final model behavior.
At the embodiment level, the effective workspace should be evaluated in terms of the positions, end-effector orientations,
and joint margins a task requires, with waist DoFs and end-effector length configured accordingly. At the control
level, body parts should be operated in parallel, compliant control should be chosen for contact tasks, and the full
human-in-the-loop path, including visual feedback, should be optimized to reduce waiting, switching, and repeated
correction. At the data level, key observations must explain the actions, collection and inference must share a consistent
state reference, and ineffective compensatory motions in demonstrations must be controlled.
These findings suggest a practical engineering path: before scaling collection, validate the embodiment’s effective
workspace, the operator’s efficiency, and the data’s learnability on representative tasks. Beyond task success,
record completion time, repeated corrections, trajectory length, and motion continuity, and check data quality through
small-scale model training and real-robot testing. Fix the system bottlenecks these reveal first, then scale up collection.
Scaling collection requires continually raising throughput, but also reducing the wasted operations and downstream
rework behind each demonstration. Improving the collection system promises to raise both data-production efficiency
and the quality of the motions models learn.
Limitations and future work.
Our experiments have limited scope. The embodiment analysis relies mainly on URDF
models, discretized IK, and representative morphologies; real workspaces are further constrained by collisions, dynamics,
and hardware limits. The control-system experiments cover a few representative tasks and a limited set of operators
and device configurations; other robot platforms and proficiency levels may yield different results. The data-quality
experiments focus on specific tasks, a single policy model, and one action representation, and some use artificially
injected offsets or a single training run. They reveal concrete data failure modes, but the magnitude and generality of
these effects deserve validation on more tasks and policy architectures. Future work will further connect collection
efficiency, trajectory quality, and model performance: under the same tasks and collection budget, we will compare data
produced by different system configurations and the policies trained on them, forming a complete evaluation pipeline
spanning collection, quality inspection, training, and real-robot acceptance.

Appendix A
Why We Use Delta Joint Actions
Feng et al. (2026) provide an empirical analysis of action-space design, highlighting the benefits of delta actions and
joint-space representations for modern policy backbones, including diffusion-based models. Motivated by these findings,
we examine delta joint actions in data collected from multiple robots with different joint zero calibrations. The following
analysis illustrates how this representation cancels constant zero offsets in action labels, while leaving their effects on
the observed state.

Common imitation-learning models condition on both images and joint state, so their behavior lies between these
two cases. In our observations, the more tokens the image occupies, the more strongly the model is driven by the
image. Consequently, predicting absolute joint targets degrades performance under zero offsets.
To prevent offsets from contaminating the action, we use delta joint actions in this study, so that the model is
affected by the offset only through the state.
